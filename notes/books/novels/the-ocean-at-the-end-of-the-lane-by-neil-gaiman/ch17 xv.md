# XV.

## 本章导航

**一句话总结**：Ginnie Hempstock 驱车送叙述者回家，抹去母亲的相关记忆；成年后叙述者收养了黑猫 Ocean——Lettie 留给他的永恒印记。

## 原文

> XV.
>
> There was a Land Rover parked behind the cowshed. The doors were open and the ignition key was in the lock. I sat on the newspaper-covered passenger seat and watched Ginnie Hempstock turn the key. The engine sputtered a few times before it started.
>
> I had not imagined any of the Hempstocks driving. I said, "I didn't know you had a car."
>
> "Lots of things you don't know," said Mrs. Hempstock, tartly. Then she glanced at me more gently and said, "You can't know everything." She backed the Land Rover up and it bumped its way forward across the ruts and the puddles of the back of the farmyard.
>
> There was something on my mind.
>
> "Old Mrs. Hempstock says that Lettie isn't really dead," I said. "But she looked dead. I think she is actually dead. I don't think it's true that she's not dead."
>
> Ginnie looked like she was going to say something about the nature of truth, but all she said was, "Lettie's hurt. Very badly hurt. The ocean has taken her. Honestly, I don't know if it will ever give her back. But we can hope, can't we?"
>
> "Yes." I squeezed my hands into fists, and I hoped as hard as I knew how.
>
> We bumped and jolted at fifteen miles per hour.
>
> I said, "Was she—is she—really your daughter?" I didn't know, I still don't know, why I asked her that. Perhaps I just wanted to know more about the girl who had saved my life, who had rescued me more than once. I didn't know anything about her.
>
> "More or less," said Ginnie. "The men Hempstocks, my brothers, they went out into the world, and they had babies who've had babies. There are Hempstock women out there in your world, and I'll wager each of them is a wonder in her own way. But only Gran and me and Lettie are the pure thing."
>
> "She didn't have a daddy?" I asked.
>
> "No."
>
> "Did you have a daddy?"
>
> "You're all questions, aren't you? No, love. We never went in for that sort of thing. You only need men if you want to breed more men."
>
> I said, "You don't have to take me home. I could stay with you. I could wait until Lettie comes back from the ocean. I could work on your farm, and carry stuff, and learn to drive a tractor."
>
> She said, "No," but she said it kindly. "You get on with your own life. Lettie gave it to you. You just have to grow up and try and be worth it."
>
> A flash of resentment. It's hard enough being alive, trying to survive in the world and find your place in it, to do the things you need to do to get by, without wondering if the thing you just did, whatever it was, was worth someone having . . . if not died, then having given up her life. It wasn't fair.
>
> "Life's not fair," said Ginnie, as if I had spoken aloud.
>
> She turned into our driveway, pulled up outside the front door. I got out and she did too.
>
> "Better make it easier for you to go home," she said.
>
> Mrs. Hempstock rang the doorbell, although the door was never locked, and industriously scraped the soles of her Wellington boots on the doormat until my mother opened the door. She was dressed for bed, and wearing her quilted pink dressing gown.
>
> "Here he is," said Ginnie. "Safe and sound, the soldier back from the wars. He had a lovely time at our Lettie's going-away party, but now it's time for this young man to get his rest."
>
> My mother looked blank—almost confused—and then the confusion was replaced by a smile, as if the world had just reconfigured itself into a form that made sense.
>
> "Oh, you didn't have to bring him back," said my mother. "One of us would have come and picked him up." Then she looked down at me. "What do you say to Mrs. Hempstock, darling?"
>
> I said it automatically.
> "Thank-you-for-having-me."
>
> My mother said, "Very good, dear." Then, "Lettie's going away?"
>
> "To Australia," said Ginnie. "To be with her father. We'll miss having this little fellow over to play, but, well, we'll let you know when Lettie comes back. He can come over and play, then."
>
> I was getting tired. The party had been fun, although I could not remember much about it. I knew that I would not visit the Hempstock farm again, though. Not unless Lettie was there.
>
> Australia was a long, long way away. I wondered how long it would be until she came back from Australia with her father. Years, I supposed. Australia was on the other side of the world, across the ocean . . .
>
> A small part of my mind remembered an alternate pattern of events and then lost it, as if I had woken from a comfortable sleep and looked around, pulled the bedclothes over me, and returned to my dream.
>
> Mrs. Hempstock got back into her ancient Land Rover, so bespattered with mud (I could now see, in the light above the front door) that there was almost no trace of the original paintwork visible, and she backed it up, down the drive, toward the lane.
>
> My mother seemed unbothered that I had returned home in fancy dress clothes at almost eleven at night. She said, "I have some bad news, dear."
>
> "What's that?"
>
> "Ursula's had to leave. Family matters. Pressing family matters. She's already left. I know how much you children liked her."
>
> I knew that I didn't like her, but I said nothing.
>
> There was now nobody sleeping in my bedroom at the top of the stairs. My mother asked if I would like my room back for a while. I said no, unsure of why I was saying no. I could not remember why I disliked Ursula Monkton so much—indeed, I felt faintly guilty for disliking her so absolutely and so irrationally—but I had no desire to return to that bedroom, despite the little yellow handbasin just my size, and I remained in the shared bedroom until our family moved out of that house half a decade later (we children protesting, the adults I think just relieved that their financial difficulties were over).
>
> The house was demolished after we moved out. I would not go and see it standing empty, and refused to witness the demolition.
> There was too much of my life bound up in those bricks and tiles, those drainpipes and walls.
>
> Years later, my sister, now an adult herself, confided in me that she believed that our mother had fired Ursula Monkton (whom she remembered, so fondly, as the only nice one in a sequence of grumpy childminders) because our father was having an affair with her. It was possible, I agreed. Our parents were still alive then, and I could have asked them, but I didn't.
>
> My father did not mention the events of those nights, not then, not later.
>
> I finally made friends with my father when I entered my twenties. We had so little in common when I was a boy, and I am certain I had been a disappointment to him. He did not ask for a child with a book, off in its own world. He wanted a son who did what he had done: swam and boxed and played rugby, and drove cars at speed with abandon and joy, but that was not what he had wound up with.
>
> I did not ever go down the lane all the way to the end. I did not think of the white Mini. When I thought of the opal miner, it was in context of the two rough raw opal-rocks that sat on our mantelpiece, and in my memory he always wore a checked shirt and jeans. His face and arms were tan, not the cherry-red of monoxide poisoning, and he had no bow-tie.
>
> Monster, the ginger tomcat the opal miner had left us, had wandered off to be fed by other families, and although we saw him, from time to time, prowling the ditches and trees at the side of the lane, he would not ever come when we called. He had never been our cat. We knew it, and so did he.
>
> A story only matters, I suspect, to the extent that the people in the story change. But I was seven when all of these things happened, and I was the same person at the end of it that I was at the beginning, wasn't I? So was everyone else. They must have been. People don't change.
>
> Some things changed, though.
>
> A month or so after the events here, and five years before the ramshackle world I lived in was demolished and replaced by trim, squat, regular houses containing smart young people who worked in the city but lived in my town, who made money by moving money from place to place but who did not build or dig or farm or weave, and nine years before I would kiss smiling Callie Anders . . .
>
> I came home from school. The month was May, or perhaps early June. She was waiting by the back door as if she knew precisely where she was and who she was looking for: a young black cat, a little larger than a kitten now, with a white splodge over one ear, and with eyes of an intense and unusual greenish-blue.
>
> She followed me into the house.
>
> I fed her with an unused can of Monster's cat food, which I spooned into Monster's dusty cat bowl.
>
> My parents, who had never noticed the ginger tom's disappearance, did not initially notice the arrival of the new kitten-cat, and by the time my father commented on her existence she had been living with us for several weeks, exploring the garden until I came home from school, then staying near me while I read or played. At night she would wait beneath the bed until the lights were turned out, then she would accommodate herself on the pillow beside me, grooming my hair, and purring, so quietly as never to disturb my sister.
>
> I would fall asleep with my face pressed into her fur, while her deep electrical purr vibrated softly against my cheek.
>
> She had such unusual eyes. They made me think of the seaside, and so I called her Ocean, and could not have told you why.

## 精读

### 1

> "Lettie's hurt. Very badly hurt. The ocean has taken her. Honestly, I don't know if it will ever give her back. But we can hope, can't we?"

**中文理解**：Lettie 受了重伤，大海把她带走了。我不知道大海会不会把她还回来。但我们可以抱有希望，不是吗？

**句子结构**：复合句。第一个分句"Lettie's hurt. Very badly hurt."是并列的短句，通过重复"hurt"制造出一种压抑的停顿感。"The ocean has taken her"使用了现在完成时，暗示一种持久的状态——Lettie 不是简单地"死去"，而是被某个更大的存在所"带走"。"Honestly, I don't know if it will ever give her back"中"give her back"将海洋拟人化，暗示它是一个有意志的主体。"But we can hope, can't we?"是反义疑问句，Ginnie 以这种方式邀请叙述者（和我）共同承担这份不确定性。

**关键词**：
- **taken**：不只是"带走"，而是一种主动的、有意图的"捕获"。"Taken"暗示一种暂时性——被带走的东西，理论上还可以被"还回来"（give back）。这与"dead"的最终性形成对比。
- **hurt**：这个选择很有分寸。"injured"太医学化，"wounded"太戏剧化，"hurt"保留了某种温柔的轻描淡写，反而更加令人心碎。

**为什么这样写**：Gaiman 没有选择直接说"Lettie is dead"或"Lettie sacrificed herself"，而是通过"the ocean has taken her"这种间接的方式。这既符合 Ginnie Hempstock 作为古老存在的智慧（她知道真相但不会强加给一个七岁孩子），也保留了叙事的不确定性——从成人叙述者的视角回望，这个句子既可以被理解为隐喻，也可以被理解为字面事实。

### 2

> "You get on with your own life. Lettie gave it to you. You just have to grow up and try and be worth it."

**中文理解**：你过好自己的日子就行。Lettie 把她的命给了你。你只需要长大，尽量让自己配得上它。

**句子结构**：三个独立的祈使句，语气从陈述事实到温和命令再到深沉的期待。"try and be worth it"中"try and"是非正式用法，暗示这不是一个可以达到的明确目标，而是一种持续的努力。

**关键词**：
- **give it to you**：这个表达直接建立了一种深重的恩债——Lettie 给了叙述者某种东西，而叙述者有责任通过活下去、通过"值得"这份给予来回应它。
- **be worth it**：这个"it"指代的是什么？是叙述者的人生，还是 Lettie 的牺牲？Gaiman 有意让这种指代保持开放。

**为什么这样写**：这句话是 Ginnie 对叙述者说的，也是叙述者在四十年后写下的这句话。这句话的核心张力在于——"life"既是一件礼物，也是一项义务。被牺牲者给了你生命，而你要用"长大"和"值得"来偿还。这为整部小说设定了成年后的反思基调。

---

### 3

> It's hard enough being alive, trying to survive in the world and find your place in it, to do the things you need to do to get by, without wondering if the thing you just did, whatever it was, was worth someone having . . . if not died, then having given up her life. It wasn't fair.

**中文理解**：活着已经够难了——在这个世界上努力生存、找到自己的位置、做那些你需要做的事来勉强过活——还要去想你刚刚做的事，不管那是什么，是否值得某个人……就算不是死了，也是放弃了她的生命。这不公平。

**句子结构**：这是一个超长的复合句，叙述者用一连串的动名词短语（"being alive, trying to survive, to do"）来堆叠生存的重量。最后通过"without wondering…was worth someone having…"这个否定结构，将"不公平"的核心释放出来——这些负担之外还要再加上一层道德债务，才是真正不公平的地方。

**关键词**：
- **being alive**：名词化，强调生命本身是一种状态，而非礼物。
- **find your place in it**：在一个充满敌意或冷漠的世界中寻找归属。
- **have given up her life**：比"died"更主动的表述——Lettie 主动选择了放弃，这是叙述者在事件发生很久以后才能理解的层次。

**为什么这样写**：这是整部小说中最直接面对"牺牲的公平性"问题的段落。叙述者（无论是作为七岁的孩子还是四十年后的成人）都在问：一个人的生命怎么能成为另一个人的"礼物"？这种不公平感是真实的，但 Ginnie 的回应——"Life's not fair"——既是一种安慰，也是一种解构：公平从来就不是宇宙的默认值。

---

### 4

> My mother looked blank—almost confused—and then the confusion was replaced by a smile, as if the world had just reconfigured itself into a form that made sense.

**中文理解**：我妈妈一脸茫然——几乎是困惑——然后困惑被一个微笑取代，仿佛世界刚刚重新组合成了一个有意义的形式。

**句子结构**：简单过去时。"as if the world had just reconfigured itself into a form that made sense"是方式状语从句，使用了虚拟语气——世界"仿佛"重新配置了，但实际上是叙述者母亲的大脑在经历认知断裂后的修复。

**关键词**：
- **blank**：母亲的空白表情意味着她的大脑经历了与叙述者睡前记忆相同的"重置"——Ursula Monkton 的记忆也被抹去了。
- **reconfigure**：技术隐喻，世界被重新配置为"makes sense"的形态——这是一种认知舒适区的重建，也暗示记忆的脆弱性。

**为什么这样写**：这个细节是小说中最令人不安的时刻之一。母亲的"空白—困惑—微笑"三段式变化，说明 Hempstock 们不仅改变了叙述者的记忆，还修改了他母亲对那一夜的理解。"The world had just reconfigured itself"这个隐喻暗示：也许"现实"本身就是可以被人为调整的，而"有意义"只是一个主观的判断标准。

### 5

> I did not ever go down the lane all the way to the end. I did not think of the white Mini. When I thought of the opal miner, it was in context of the two rough raw opal-rocks that sat on our mantelpiece, and in my memory he always wore a checked shirt and jeans. His face and arms were tan, not the cherry-red of monoxide poisoning, and he had no bow-tie.

**中文理解**：我再也没有走到小巷的尽头。我没有想到那辆白色 Mini。当我想起那个欧泊矿工时，脑海里浮现的是壁炉架上那两块粗糙的生欧泊石，在我的记忆里，他总是穿着格子衬衫和牛仔裤。他的脸和手臂是棕褐色的，而不是煤气中毒的樱桃红色，而且他没有领结。

**句子结构**：五个独立的否定陈述句，每一句都在清理记忆中的"异物"。"When I thought of the opal miner"引出一个对比：记忆中的矿工（无害的、具体的）与实际的矿工（怪物般的、致命的）形成对比。

**关键词**：
- **all the way to the end**：小巷的尽头是叙述者童年创伤的核心地点，但叙述者主动回避了它——这是一种生存策略。
- **checked shirt and jeans**：具体的、平凡的细节，与怪物的形象形成对比。叙述者在无意识地改写记忆，将怪物还原为"普通人"。
- **cherry-red of monoxide poisoning**：这是原文中最精确的记忆"篡改"——叙述者用煤气中毒的典型症状（面色樱桃红）来否认怪物的真实本质。值得注意的是，这个描述本身就包含了一个医学事实——但叙述者用它来否认自己亲眼所见的恐怖。

**为什么这样写**：这段是整部小说对"记忆如何保护我们"最深刻的反思。叙述者成年后主动选择不去小巷尽头、不去想白色 Mini——这些是弗洛伊德式的防御机制（压抑）。而对欧泊矿工的记忆改写则更为复杂：叙述者通过"把他放在欧泊石的语境中"来将他驯化，用平凡的外表取代了他作为怪物的真实形象。"He had no bow-tie"是点睛之笔——领结是怪物身份的标记，去掉它就等于抹去了那段记忆。

### 6

> A story only matters, I suspect, to the extent that the people in the story change. But I was seven when all of these things happened, and I was the same person at the end of it that I was at the beginning, wasn't I? So was everyone else. They must have been. People don't change.

**中文理解**：我想，一个故事只有当故事里的人发生变化时才有意义。但发生这一切的时候我只有七岁，而在结束时我还是那个开始时的我，不是吗？其他人也一样。他们一定也是。人不改变。

**句子结构**：这是叙述者对自己童年的哲学性反思。"A story only matters…"是全称判断，但随后被"was seven"的经验事实所否定——七岁的他太小了，小到还来不及改变就被冻结在了创伤里。最后三句是一个自我对话：问题（"wasn't I?"），断言（"So was everyone else"），和最后的结论（"People don't change"）——但这个结论的语气（must have been / don't）暗示了这更像是防御性的推理，而非确定的知识。

**关键词**：
- **change**：这个词是这段话的核心。叙述者后来发现，人确实会改变——他与父亲在二十岁时和解，他找到了爱情（Callie Anders），他收养了 Ocean——但在七岁那个时间点，他无法看到这些。
- **People don't change**：这是一个绝望的陈述，也是一个悬而未决的设问。整部小说都在反驳它——叙述者从七岁走到了中年，Ocean 从小猫长成了大猫，而 Lettie 在海洋中沉睡、疗愈、等待。

**为什么这样写**：这段话的结构非常精妙：先提出一个哲学命题，然后用经验来质疑它，最后用一个不确定的结论来维持张力。"People don't change"作为自我保护的最后防线——如果人不会改变，那么七岁时发生的事就无法真正影响"真正的我"。但这恰恰是叙述者所需要的幻觉，因为改变意味着承认失去。

### 7

> A young black cat, a little larger than a kitten now, with a white splodge over one ear, and with eyes of an intense and unusual greenish-blue.

**中文理解**：一只小黑猫，比小猫大不了多少，一只耳朵上有一块白斑，眼睛是浓郁的、罕见的绿蓝色。

**句子结构**：名词短语，无动词。这是一种纯粹的描述性语言，通过细节的精确性来制造存在感：黑色、白色斑块、绿蓝色眼睛——每一个颜色词都在呼应前文建立的符号体系（Ocean = Lettie 的遗产）。

**关键词**：
- **greenish-blue**：这个颜色形容词组是整个小说中最有分量的一组颜色词。绿蓝色是海洋的颜色，也是叙述者看到就会想起海边的颜色——因为 Ocean 的眼睛，他想起了海边，但他"无法告诉你为什么"（could not have told you why）。这是压抑机制的核心证据。
- **splodge**：不规则的色块。Random House Dictionary定义为"a large, irregular spot or patch"，这个词比"spot"更粗糙、更有机，暗示这不是一只"普通"的猫。

**为什么这样写**：这只猫的出现是小说中最温柔的闭合方式。叙述者没有去找她——她就在那里等着，像是她知道他会来。绿蓝色的眼睛是海洋的颜色，是 Lettie 的颜色，是叙述者已经压抑到无意识中但仍然存在于身体里的记忆。"could not have told you why"——他不知道为什么给她取名 Ocean，但实际上他完全知道，只是他压抑了那个理由。

### 8

> I would fall asleep with my face pressed into her fur, while her deep electrical purr vibrated softly against my cheek.

**中文理解**：我会把脸埋进她的毛里睡着，而她低沉的、像电流一样的呼噜声会轻轻地震动在我的脸颊上。

**句子结构**：并列复合句。前半部分"with my face pressed into her fur"是伴随状语，后半部分"while her deep electrical purr vibrated softly against my cheek"引入了时间并列，强调这个动作的重复性和持续性。

**关键词**：
- **pressed**：这个动词暗示了一种主动的亲近——不只是"靠着"，而是把脸"压进"毛里，是一种寻找温暖的原始动作。
- **electrical**：这个词在这个语境中格外引人注目。Gaiman 曾在第一章用过" electrical Uncle"来形容怪物对煤矿的感知。这不是普通的比喻——这个"电流般的"呼噜声可能暗示了 Hempstock 魔法的本质，也可能暗示了 Ocean 与海洋的某种深层联系。
- **vibrated**：呼噜声的物理效果——不是"sound"而是"vibrate"，强调的是触感而非听觉。

**为什么这样写**："electrical purr"是整个小说最温柔的意象之一，它同时也是最令人不安的之一——因为我们刚刚在"electrical Uncle"的语境中经历过"electrical"的另一层含义。这两个"electrical"的并置不是巧合：它暗示了 Ocean 与那个宇宙恐怖之间的某种联系，但这种联系在爱与温暖的语境中被完全驯化了。这也许就是 Gaiman 的核心主题：爱可以驯化恐怖，或者至少给恐怖一个新的面孔。
