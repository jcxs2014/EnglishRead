# 27. IX

## 本章导航

**一句话概括**：Mr Scrooge, sir! I say, sir – Mr Scrooge! In the years that had passed since the events of that most remarkable Christmas Eve, Ebenezer had become accustomed to the existence in his life of what one might call a kind of benevolent coincidence. Whereas, in his former career, for all that he strove and bludgeoned, for all that he set his formidable will against the world, he always thought his days to be effortful things and – in spite of his success in the squeezing of money from the poor and the unfortunate – he never found that any piece of good chance ever dropped in his lap nor that any unexpected blessing ever arrived without warning. Since his change, however, such things were a common occurrence. Often he would encounter the right stranger at the right time, be in the right place to be able to do some lasting good to another, and even to overhear some happy news or good opinion. Kindness, he had discovered, begat kindness, and to work in the service of the light brought only illumination. All of which is but preamble to declare that, as Scrooge walked back to the Club, following his meeting with the widower, Trelawney, he heard his own name being called out from the other side of the street. "Mr Scrooge, sir! Wait there!" The old man looked across the bustling thoroughfare and saw, between the darting cabs and shoals of pedestrians, the familiar figure of Mr Barwood – the head butler at the house of Mr Talbot. He generally struck one as a solid and rather sombre figure, but this morning he was, for some reason, all movement and agitation. He waved energetically once again, although he must certainly have realised that he had by then obtained the attention of Mr Scrooge. "Stay there, sir!" he called again. "Let me come to you, sir!" And, without waiting for any gesture or encouragement, Mr Barwood moved with surprising speed and dexterity across the street, dodging around traffic of all kinds, before fetching up by Ebenezer's side, without showing the least sign of exertion. "Mr Scrooge, sir, what a pleasant surprise," he said. "I thought it must be you, sir. I'd know you anywhere! I just had to come across and say good day to you and ensure that you are quite recovered after last night." "Well, it's a fine thing indeed to see you again, Mr Barwood," said Scrooge, which was at least a partial truth, for all that he was in no particular mood for the trading of polite conversation. "But what do you mean when you ask if I'm recovered?" "Oh, I don't mind admitting I was a little worried about you, sir. Truly I don't. After the voice that came through Mr Beck, sir, and the effect it had upon you." Scrooge looked levelly into the kind eyes of the butler. "It was something of a shock, Mr Barwood. Yes. Though the delivery of shock is rather Mr Beck's stock-in-trade, wouldn't you say?" "Forgive me, sir, for I know he is a proud man who admires himself a deal too much, but I helped Mr Beck into his cab last night after the evening took the turn that it did and you should know, Mr Scrooge, sir, that the fellow was in a bad way. He looked appalled at what he'd done, sir, and more than that, a little afraid too. He was muttering to himself, sir, and looking all around him, for all the world, sir, as though he were a felon on the loose." "Ah," said Mr Scrooge, and: "is that so?" "Yes, sir, it surely is." "Well, such oddities are to be expected, I suppose, in his line of work. And, indeed, in mine. Now, I must away, Mr Barwood, a pleasure though it is to see you again. I have to return to my Club, and travel at once to the country." "Oh," said Mr Barwood, seeming oddly disconsolate at the news. "Is it a trip for pleasure, Mr Scrooge?" "More in a capacity of providing help if I can," said Scrooge. "To that same young lady, in fact, whose acquaintance you made last night. Mrs Fell." "Ah," said Mr Barwood. "Of course." He seemed rather far away now. "Well, good luck to you both. I hope to see you again before long, sir, at another of Mr Talbot's sittings." Scrooge said that they would just have to see about that, after which the two men said their goodbyes and were about to go their separate ways when Barwood called out after the old man: "Mr Scrooge, sir? This place in the country… it wouldn't be called Starningham, now, would it?" Ebenezer stopped. He looked round at the butler. "It would, Mr Barwood, yes. Now how did you know that?" "It was a name that Mr Beck kept saying last night, sir, as I bundled him into that cab. It was a name he was saying over and over. And with an awful kind of relish, too." He paused, as though he were uncertain as to whether he should say more or not. In the end he said: "You'll be careful, Mr Scrooge, sir, won't you? If you do go there. You'll watch your step?" "Why do you say such a thing, Mr Barwood?" "Not sure, sir," said the butler. "I expect it's nothing. But somehow I felt I had to say it." Then he turned and melted back into the London crowds, while Mr Scrooge – troubled – walked on.

## 一句话总结

第二十七章（Stave Three IX）：Mr Scrooge, sir! I say, sir – Mr Scrooge! In the years that had passed since the events of that most remarkable Christmas Eve, Ebenezer had become accustomed to the existence in his life of what one might call a kind of benevolent coincidence. Whereas, in his former career, for all that he strove and bludgeoned, for all that he set his formidable will against the world, he always thought his days to be effortful things and – in spite of his success in the squeezing of money from the poor and the unfortunate – he never found that any piece of good chance ever dropped in his lap nor that any unexpected blessing ever arrived without warning. Since his change, however, such things were a common occurrence. Often he would encounter the right stranger at the right time, be in the right place to be able to do some lasting good to another, and even to overhear some happy news or good opinion. Kindness, he had discovered, begat kindness, and to work in the service of the light brought only illumination. All of which is but preamble to declare that, as Scrooge walked back to the Club, following his meeting with the widower, Trelawney, he heard his own name being called out from the other side of the street. "Mr Scrooge, sir! Wait there!" The old man looked across the bustling thoroughfare and saw, between the darting cabs and shoals of pedestrians, the familiar figure of Mr Barwood – the head butler at the house of Mr Talbot. He generally struck one as a solid and rather sombre figure, but this morning he was, for some reason, all movement and agitation. He waved energetically once again, although he must certainly have realised that he had by then obtained the attention of Mr Scrooge. "Stay there, sir!" he called again. "Let me come to you, sir!" And, without waiting for any gesture or encouragement, Mr Barwood moved with surprising speed and dexterity across the street, dodging around traffic of all kinds, before fetching up by Ebenezer's side, without showing the least sign of exertion. "Mr Scrooge, sir, what a pleasant surprise," he said. "I thought it must be you, sir. I'd know you anywhere! I just had to come across and say good day to you and ensure that you are quite recovered after last night." "Well, it's a fine thing indeed to see you again, Mr Barwood," said Scrooge, which was at least a partial truth, for all that he was in no particular mood for the trading of polite conversation. "But what do you mean when you ask if I'm recovered?" "Oh, I don't mind admitting I was a little worried about you, sir. Truly I don't. After the voice that came through Mr Beck, sir, and the effect it had upon you." Scrooge looked levelly into the kind eyes of the butler. "It was something of a shock, Mr Barwood. Yes. Though the delivery of shock is rather Mr Beck's stock-in-trade, wouldn't you say?" "Forgive me, sir, for I know he is a proud man who admires himself a deal too much, but I helped Mr Beck into his cab last night after the evening took the turn that it did and you should know, Mr Scrooge, sir, that the fellow was in a bad way. He looked appalled at what he'd done, sir, and more than that, a little afraid too. He was muttering to himself, sir, and looking all around him, for all the world, sir, as though he were a felon on the loose." "Ah," said Mr Scrooge, and: "is that so?" "Yes, sir, it surely is." "Well, such oddities are to be expected, I suppose, in his line of work. And, indeed, in mine. Now, I must away, Mr Barwood, a pleasure though it is to see you again. I have to return to my Club, and travel at once to the country." "Oh," said Mr Barwood, seeming oddly disconsolate at the news. "Is it a trip for pleasure, Mr Scrooge?" "More in a capacity of providing help if I can," said Scrooge. "To that same young lady, in fact, whose acquaintance you made last night. Mrs Fell." "Ah," said Mr Barwood. "Of course." He seemed rather far away now. "Well, good luck to you both. I hope to see you again before long, sir, at another of Mr Talbot's sittings." Scrooge said that they would just have to see about that, after which the two men said their goodbyes and were about to go their separate ways when Barwood called out after the old man: "Mr Scrooge, sir? This place in the country… it wouldn't be called Starningham, now, would it?" Ebenezer stopped. He looked round at the butler. "It would, Mr Barwood, yes. Now how did you know that?" "It was a name that Mr Beck kept saying last night, sir, as I bundled him into that cab. It was a name he was saying over and over. And with an awful kind of relish, too." He paused, as though he were uncertain as to whether he should say more or not. In the end he said: "You'll be careful, Mr Scrooge, sir, won't you? If you do go there. You'll watch your step?" "Why do you say such a thing, Mr Barwood?" "Not sure, sir," said the butler. "I expect it's nothing. But somehow I felt I had to say it."
