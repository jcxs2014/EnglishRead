# 37. V

## 本章导航

**一句话概括**：The house, although it had seemed big to Louisa in childhood, was not a terribly large one. There were four rooms on the ground floor and as many again on the second. The third appeared to be comprised, for reasons which seemed obscure, of a single chamber. Not, of course, that any of them ventured so far upstairs in the first instance. While Mrs Fell and the captain stood in the hallway, Mrs Bethlem bustled past them both. For what she suspected would surely be the final time, she set about lighting the lamps in the house before ushering the two new arrivals into the drawing room, a snug little space with table and chairs, which was the nearest thing that the building possessed to a space of comfort. She addressed the lady and the gentlemen then, giving them a speech into which she had poured a good deal of thought and consideration. "Now, I dare say you both have a deal of questions. I will answer them all as best I can. But before then, I want you to understand one important fact." Her tone was that of the schoolmistress insisting upon the attention of some recalcitrant. "Of course." "Well, then. The fact is this: it is quite possible to spend time in this house and to live here – even, I dare say, to raise a family here – without being in the slightest danger or being in the least unsettled. I have no doubt that you know the house's reputation and even that of its owner, your uncle. And, having worked in and around the place for a number of years now, it is my considered opinion that this house is indeed what country folk would think of as 'haunted'. As to what forces may or may not be at work here, I leave that to men of the cloth and to men of learning. Yet it is my certain belief that you may be happy here, Mrs Fell, if only you follow these three rules." She paused at this point, not only for reasons of dramatic effect but also to catch her breath and to take a swift look at the faces of her audience. To her great relief, they seemed to be listening attentively and to be receiving her words with all necessary seriousness. It was a good thing, thought Mrs Bethlem, that both had open minds; a pair of sceptics or scoffers would be placing themselves in the path of considerable peril simply by setting foot into the house at all. "Rule one," said the lady, "is do not open the front door to anybody after ten o'clock at night, no matter who you think is waiting for you there." "But it might be you, Mrs Bethlem," said the man, trying to be jocular. "We would so hate to leave you shivering on the doorstep." The woman's voice was grave. "You do not open the door even to me, Captain Ambrose. Or – I should say – even to what seems to you to resemble me." She looked at him then till the old soldier lowered his gaze. "The second rule," she went on, "is to ignore any sounds you hear from the uppermost floor of this house. Indeed, my advice is to avoid venturing up there altogether unless it is absolutely necessary. You may from time to time hear sounds which might resemble… banging. Or running feet. Or a kind of rustling sound, like the wind through dry leaves. Now, you leave these things alone. In this, I wish to make myself clear. You do not investigate and you leave well alone. The noises will stop in the end. They always do." "But I might need that room," said Mrs Fell stoutly. "It seems an awful pity to leave a whole floor of a house like this entirely empty." "You can take it from me," said Mrs Bethlem, "that you will regret any decision you might take to make of that upper floor anything useful or decorative. Leave it be, Mrs Fell, that's my strong advice to you. This will never be a large household. You have no room for servants. And even if you did, you would find no one local who would accept the position. I have made arrangements for a young girl from the village – one Susan Naylor – to come into the house every morning and to stay until luncheon, so as to make sure that you have everything you need. If the arrangement is not to your taste, then you are quite at liberty to make your own. I have promised the girl nothing." "I am obliged to you," said Louisa. "Not at all. I wanted your first days here to be as… easy as possible." "That was thoughtful of you." Mrs Fell smiled at the older woman, a gesture which was not then returned in kind. "The third and final rule," Bethlem went on, "is the most important of all. I would do everything in your power to avoid the asking of questions when you are together or alone in this house." "I'm not sure I understand what you mean by that." A note of bemusement had entered the voice of Captain Ambrose. "I mean exactly what I say, sir. I mean the asking of questions of any sort." "But what happens," asked Louisa Fell, "if any questions are inadvertently asked?" The other woman did not reply to this at first. Instead, her eyes darted quickly around the room and she seemed to be listening out for something either above them or in the adjacent rooms. There was only silence. "Well," she said, "the house has a tendency to… answer. Not always. But often. And the answers that it gives may not be welcome ones." The captain and Mrs Fell exchanged a glance at this. Mrs Bethlem, meanwhile, seemed relieved to be coming to the end of her speech. "And that, I think, is everything of substance. Now, let me show you everything you ought to see before I take my leave of you." "You are most welcome to stay with us," said Mrs Fell. "Since you were kind enough to have arranged food for our larder, it's really the least we can do to share it with you." "Thank you, that is most kind. But I have no wish to eat here." "I see." "Now, let me show you the rest of the house." Without waiting for any response, Bethlem swept from the room, in the evident expectation that the others would follow, which indeed they did. From somewhere outside, a dog barked three times, then fell silent.

第三十七章（Stave Three V）：The house, although it had seemed big to Louisa in childhood, was not a terribly large one. There were four rooms on the ground floor and as many again on the second. The third appeared to be comprised, for reasons which seemed obscure, of a single chamber. Not, of course, that any of them ventured so far upstairs in the first instance. While Mrs Fell and the captain stood in the hallway, Mrs Bethlem bustled past them both. For what she suspected would surely be the final time, she set about lighting the lamps in the house before ushering the two new arrivals into the drawing room, a snug little space with table and chairs, which was the nearest thing that the building possessed to a space of comfort. She addressed the lady and the gentlemen then, giving them a speech into which she had poured a good deal of thought and consideration. "Now, I dare say you both have a deal of questions. I will answer them all as best I can. But before then, I want you to understand one important fact." Her tone was that of the schoolmistress insisting upon the attention of some recalcitrant. "Of course." "Well, then. The fact is this: it is quite possible to spend time in this house and to live here – even, I dare say, to raise a family here – without being in the slightest danger or being in the least unsettled. I have no doubt that you know the house's reputation and even that of its owner, your uncle. And, having worked in and around the place for a number of years now, it is my considered opinion that this house is indeed what country folk would think of as 'haunted'. As to what forces may or may not be at work here, I leave that to men of the cloth and to men of learning. Yet it is my certain belief that you may be happy here, Mrs Fell, if only you follow these three rules." She paused at this point, not only for reasons of dramatic effect but also to catch her breath and to take a swift look at the faces of her audience. To her great relief, they seemed to be listening attentively and to be receiving her words with all necessary seriousness. It was a good thing, thought Mrs Bethlem, that both had open minds; a pair of sceptics or scoffers would be placing themselves in the path of considerable peril simply by setting foot into the house at all. "Rule one," said the lady, "is do not open the front door to anybody after ten o'clock at night, no matter who you think is waiting for you there." "But it might be you, Mrs Bethlem," said the man, trying to be jocular. "We would so hate to leave you shivering on the doorstep." The woman's voice was grave. "You do not open the door even to me, Captain Ambrose. Or – I should say – even to what seems to you to resemble me." She looked at him then till the old soldier lowered his gaze. "The second rule," she went on, "is to ignore any sounds you hear from the uppermost floor of this house. Indeed, my advice is to avoid venturing up there altogether unless it is absolutely necessary. You may from time to time hear sounds which might resemble… banging. Or running feet. Or a kind of rustling sound, like the wind through dry leaves. Now, you leave these things alone. In this, I wish to make myself clear. You do not investigate and you leave well alone. The noises will stop in the end. They always do." "But I might need that room," said Mrs Fell stoutly. "It seems an awful pity to leave a whole floor of a house like this entirely empty." "You can take it from me," said Mrs Bethlem, "that you will regret any decision you might take to make of that upper floor anything useful or decorative. Leave it be, Mrs Fell, that's my strong advice to you. This will never be a large household. You have no room for servants. And even if you did, you would find no one local who would accept the position. I have made arrangements for a young girl from the village – one Susan Naylor – to come into the house every morning and to stay until luncheon, so as to make sure that you have everything you need. I wanted your first days here to be as… easy as possible." "That was thoughtful of you." Mrs Fell smiled at the older woman, a gesture which was not then returned in kind. "The third and final rule," Bethlem went on, "is the most important of all. I would do everything in your power to avoid the asking of questions when you are together or alone in this house." "I'm not sure I understand what you mean by that." A note of bemusement had entered the voice of Captain Ambrose. "I mean exactly what I say, sir. I mean the asking of questions of any sort." "But what happens," asked Louisa Fell, "if any questions are inadvertently asked?" The other woman did not reply to this at first. Instead, her eyes darted quickly around the room and she seemed to be listening out for something either above them or in the adjacent rooms. There was only silence. "Well," she said, "the house has a tendency to… answer. Not always. But often. And the answers that it gives may not be welcome ones." The captain and Mrs Fell exchanged a glance at this. Mrs Bethlem, meanwhile, seemed relieved to be coming to the end of her speech. "And that, I think, is everything of substance. Now, let me show you everything you ought to see before I take my leave of you." "You are most welcome to stay with us," said Mrs Fell. "Since you were kind enough to have arranged food for our larder, it's really the least we can do to share it with you." "Thank you, that is most kind. But I have no wish to eat here." "I see." "Now, let me show you the rest of the house." Without waiting for any response, Bethlem swept from the room, in the evident expectation that the others would follow, which indeed they did.
