# 35. III

## 本章导航

**一句话概括**：Night had started to fall by the time Mrs Louisa Fell and Captain Ambrose finally arrived in Starningham. The train had reached its terminus in the city of Norwich, and from there the gentleman and lady had secured the services of a coach and driver who had taken them the remaining fifteen miles. It was a journey across stark, wintry countryside, beside fields and patches of woodland, and against a gathering twilight. For Mrs Fell, the trip, most naturally, brought back memories of her last visit to this secluded portion of England in the company of her late father. For the captain, who – although he was, by any standards, a well-travelled man – had never before visited the county, it was nonetheless curiously familiar, as though he had somehow seen this flat, low landscape before, perhaps in some half-remembered dream. It was, however, the sky which he would always remember from that journey – that great, grey and glowering sky which filled up the horizon as if to remind those who laboured beneath it of their own sublime insignificance when weighed against the scale of the world. There was little opportunity for conversation as the coach rattled through this bleak and often minatory countryside. Although it must be said that both the gentleman and the lady exchanged frequent glances. These spoke of strength, understanding and determination, and they only grew in number as the pair approached their destination. They both felt a sense of awful anticipation, a terrible sense of inevitability as the miles fell away beneath the hooves of the horses and the wheels of the carriage, as the thought of Starningham became an actuality. And then, at last, they were there, the long journey done with (though, as Mrs Fell remarked, it was a very much more swift undertaking than it had been in her childhood, before the coming of the railways). By this time, their arrival betokened no greater emotion than relief, and they were happy indeed to climb down and stretch themselves a little and take in the crisp, clear air of the place. They alighted by the little marketplace which was, at this hour, in the process of being closed up. From here, they thanked their driver and walked down in the direction of the church. Had the nature of their expedition not been such a disagreeable one, they might have found the place to be charming and quaint. To walk the streets of Starningham was to walk through a picture of England which had long since vanished in the big cities. In addition to these observations, it ought also to be noted that Starningham was not a place in which it would be easy to lose one's bearings, for all of the streets seemed to emanate from the church, like spokes from a cartwheel, and the spire of that place of worship was visible at all times. Mrs Fell and the captain walked to the church which, being built of pale French sandstone, was a fine example of the Norman style. For all of that, however, it had a sense both of benevolent antiquity and of having been well cared for in all the years of its existence, which provided a source of considerable comfort to the two visitors. As they approached the structure, Captain Ambrose was suddenly overcome by a mad desire to seize the hand of his companion and walk with her to the gates of the church in the manner of young sweethearts. This absurdity he pushed aside at once, of course, and restricted himself to a single question: "Are you quite well, madam?" She smiled, perhaps at the rather odd formality of his tone. "Quite well, thank you, Captain. If a little tired. I shall be glad when we are in the house and there is an opportunity for rest." "I too, ma'am." "No regrets, I hope? At having come all this way?" Ambrose shook his head. "No, ma'am." "I'm glad." And then, as if she had somehow heard his earlier thought, she offered him her hand to take. He took it, without thinking. And it was in this fashion that they reached the front of the church. "Let us hope that we can find this Mrs Bethlem as soon as possible and, well, see what we can see in… my inheritance." They stepped into the church. Inside it was cool and dry and rather welcoming. There was a distinctive scent in the air, of dust and of age and of candle wax. No service was in progress and there was but one other person visible: a man of fifty or so, dressed in a cassock, who was busying himself around the altar. At the sight of the new arrivals, he hurried over to greet them. "Welcome, welcome." He was smiling, friendly, yet both Ambrose and Mrs Fell were later to agree that there was an odd nervousness to his manner too, something almost skittish underneath. "You are visitors, I think, to our little town?" Captain Ambrose agreed that they were, at which the priest bade them welcome once again. Mrs Fell ventured to say how pleasant the place was, and she was about to ask as to the whereabouts of Mrs Bethlem when the priest cut across her words with the blunt question: "Why are you here, ma'am?" "I have a house here," said the lady firmly. "I've come to claim it." When the man spoke again, there was a fracture in his voice. "Brightwell Road?" "That's correct," said Mrs Fell, and she was about to ask him how he had guessed it so easily when the priest spoke again. "You shouldn't…" he began, "you shouldn't have come here." Of course, the lady was about to ask him why, and Captain Ambrose meant to tell him in no uncertain terms that he really ought not to be alarming her unduly, when a fresh voice called out. "Mrs Fell? It is you, isn't it?" They all looked over at this and saw that a stranger had arrived, and was even then scurrying with distinct self-importance in their direction. She was a stranger to them but not, entirely, to us, for we have seen her before, locking up the house on Brightwell Road. "I'm Mrs Bethlem," she said, and extended her hand. "You must be Mrs Fell. And this is…" She looked over at Captain Ambrose, at which a flicker of what looked very much like relief passed across her face. The captain introduced himself and he too shook hands with the lady. "You're both very welcome here," she said. "I can see you've been admiring our church. My late father was the Sacristan here. It's a beautiful place and a great place of sanctuary too. If you should ever need it." "Why should the lady need sanctuary?" asked the captain. Mrs Bethlem laughed, most unconvincingly. "Merely a figure of speech," she said. "Now then, Mrs Fell, I understand that all of the necessary paperwork has already been completed in London with Mr Cadge of Cleeford, Crawlin and Cadge." "That is so," said Mrs Fell. "In which case, this key is yours." With a flourish, she produced from about her person the great iron key to the last house on Brightwell Road. "Would you like to see what you have inherited?" "Of course I should," said the lady. "Good, good!" Bethlem had already turned and was striding back towards the door. "This way!" The captain and Mrs Fell began to follow, but as she turned away, the lady felt a hand upon her shoulder. It was the priest, of course, who now looked to be in a state not far from pain. "You can always come here," he said. "You can always come back. It's safe. I promise." "Come along!" Mrs Bethlem was evidently growing impatient. "Thank you," was all that Louisa Fell said to the priest, then she and the captain were outside again, trailing behind their host, as she strode away from the shadow of the church and towards the street beyond.

第三十五章（Stave Three III）：Night had started to fall by the time Mrs Louisa Fell and Captain Ambrose finally arrived in Starningham. The train had reached its terminus in the city of Norwich, and from there the gentleman and lady had secured the services of a coach and driver who had taken them the remaining fifteen miles. It was a journey across stark, wintry countryside, beside fields and patches of woodland, and against a gathering twilight. For Mrs Fell, the trip, most naturally, brought back memories of her last visit to this secluded portion of England in the company of her late father. For the captain, who – although he was, by any standards, a well-travelled man – had never before visited the county, it was nonetheless curiously familiar, as though he had somehow seen this flat, low landscape before, perhaps in some half-remembered dream. It was, however, the sky which he would always remember from that journey – that great, grey and glowering sky which filled up the horizon as if to remind those who laboured beneath it of their own sublime insignificance when weighed against the scale of the world. There was little opportunity for conversation as the coach rattled through this bleak and often minatory countryside. Although it must be said that both the gentleman and the lady exchanged frequent glances. These spoke of strength, understanding and determination, and they only grew in number as the pair approached their destination. They both felt a sense of awful anticipation, a terrible sense of inevitability as the miles fell away beneath the hooves of the horses and the wheels of the carriage, as the thought of Starningham became an actuality. And then, at last, they were there, the long journey done with (though, as Mrs Fell remarked, it was a very much more swift undertaking than it had been in her childhood, before the coming of the railways). By this time, their arrival betokened no greater emotion than relief, and they were happy indeed to climb down and stretch themselves a little and take in the crisp, clear air of the place. They alighted by the little marketplace which was, at this hour, in the process of being closed up. From here, they thanked their driver and walked down in the direction of the church. Had the nature of their expedition not been such a disagreeable one, they might have found the place to be charming and quaint. To walk the streets of Starningham was to walk through a picture of England which had long since vanished in the big cities. In addition to these observations, it ought also to be noted that Starningham was not a place in which it would be easy to lose one's bearings, for all of the streets seemed to emanate from the church, like spokes from a cartwheel, and the spire of that place of worship was visible at all times. Mrs Fell and the captain walked to the church which, being built of pale French sandstone, was a fine example of the Norman style. For all of that, however, it had a sense both of benevolent antiquity and of having been well cared for in all the years of its existence, which provided a source of considerable comfort to the two visitors. As they approached the structure, Captain Ambrose was suddenly overcome by a mad desire to seize the hand of his companion and walk with her to the gates of the church in the manner of young sweethearts. This absurdity he pushed aside at once, of course, and restricted himself to a single question: "Are you quite well, madam?" She smiled, perhaps at the rather odd formality of his tone. "Quite well, thank you, Captain. If a little tired. I shall be glad when we are in the house and there is an opportunity for rest." "I too, ma'am." "No regrets, I hope? At having come all this way?" Ambrose shook his head. "No, ma'am." "I'm glad." And then, as if she had somehow heard his earlier thought, she offered him her hand to take. He took it, without thinking. And it was in this fashion that they reached the front of the church. "Let us hope that we can find this Mrs Bethlem as soon as possible and, well, see what we can see in… my inheritance." They stepped into the church. Inside it was cool and dry and rather welcoming. There was a distinctive scent in the air, of dust and of age and of candle wax. No service was in progress and there was but one other person visible: a man of fifty or so, dressed in a cassock, who was busying himself around the altar. At the sight of the new arrivals, he hurried over to greet them. "Welcome, welcome." He was smiling, friendly, yet both Ambrose and Mrs Fell were later to agree that there was an odd nervousness to his manner too, something almost skittish underneath. "You are visitors, I think, to our little town?" Captain Ambrose agreed that they were, at which the priest bade them welcome once again. Mrs Fell ventured to say how pleasant the place was, and she was about to ask as to the whereabouts of Mrs Bethlem when the priest cut across her words with the blunt question: "Why are you here, ma'am?" "I have a house here," said the lady firmly. "I've come to claim it." When the man spoke again, there was a fracture in his voice. "Brightwell Road?" "That's correct," said Mrs Fell, and she was about to ask him how he had guessed it so easily when the priest spoke again. "You shouldn't…" he began, "you shouldn't have come here." Of course, the lady was about to ask him why, and Captain Ambrose meant to tell him in no uncertain terms that he really ought not to be alarming her unduly, when a fresh voice called out. "Mrs Fell? It is you, isn't it?" They all looked over at this and saw that a stranger had arrived, and was even then scurrying with distinct self-importance in their direction. She was a stranger to them but not, entirely, to us, for we have seen her before, locking up the house on Brightwell Road. "I'm Mrs Bethlem," she said, and extended her hand. "You must be Mrs Fell. And this is…" She looked over at Captain Ambrose, at which a flicker of what looked very much like relief passed across her face. The captain introduced himself and he too shook hands with the lady. "You're both very welcome here," she said. "I can see you've been admiring our church. My late father was the Sacristan here. It's a beautiful place and a great place of sanctuary too. If you should ever need it." "Why should the lady need sanctuary?" asked the captain. Mrs Bethlem laughed, most unconvincingly. "Merely a figure of speech," she said. "Now then, Mrs Fell, I understand that all of the necessary paperwork has already been completed in London with Mr Cadge of Cleeford, Crawlin and Cadge." "That is so," said Mrs Fell. "In which case, this key is yours." With a flourish, she produced from about her person the great iron key to the last house on Brightwell Road. "Would you like to see what you have inherited?" "Of course I should," said the lady. "Good, good!" Bethlem had already turned and was striding back towards the door. "This way!" The captain and Mrs Fell began to follow, but as she turned away, the lady felt a hand upon her shoulder. It was the priest, of course, who now looked to be in a state not far from pain. "You can always come here," he said. "You can always come back. It's safe. I promise." "Come along!" Mrs Bethlem was evidently growing impatient.
