# 28. X

## 本章导航

**一句话概括**："I suppose," said Captain Ambrose, reaching the crescendo of his tale, "that we had meant to set a kind of trap for the spectre that was pursuing poor Halvorsen. We gave the man, as I have said, an excellent luncheon and bade him rest awhile beneath our roof. The day stayed very hot and the man was quite happy to sit, rather listlessly, in the library and leaf through a volume or two of seafaring tales which Mr Scrooge had produced from some obscure shelf or other. It was only much later, after more food and a little wine, and once dusk had begun to fall, that we said our goodbyes at the entrance to the Club and watched Mr Halvorsen leave and begin his walk through to the city to where he would be staying that night, in an establishment on Goodge Street. "All of these farewells were done with some theatricality, as though we meant to fool some eavesdropper or spectator. The fellow departed, seeming happy enough, and we waited awhile on the threshold, enjoying the relative cool of the evening after the baking heat of the day. Then – as we had agreed some hours before – Mr Scrooge and I began to follow Mr Halvorsen, stepping discreetly behind him, keeping the fellow always in view but also maintaining a respectable distance." "You meant, I suppose," said Mrs Fell, "to see for yourselves if this apparition would take shape behind Mr Halvorsen? Or else to make certain that the ghost wasn't just a real person after all, who would traipse behind the sailor all night long?" "Exactly right," said Captain Ambrose. "Or – quite frankly, madam – to see if there was anything there at all. Or if the whole thing wasn't entirely in the fellow's head." "So what happened?" "Well, for a long time, at least for an hour or so, we had begun to believe that this was by far the likeliest explanation. There was no sign of any spirit nor of any curious living man. For Halvorsen walked through the streets entirely unaccosted. He took the route that you yourself no doubt took yesterday when you visited the Club, walking up through Piccadilly Circus and then to the streets beyond, moving up towards the Tottenham Court Road. In all this time, the only people that were following Mr Halvorsen were Mr Scrooge and myself. We were about halfway along the length of Tottenham Court Road, at which point Mr Halvorsen had turned right, down a smaller, narrower street, rather poorly lit and with fewer pedestrians, when something strange began to happen. At first, I thought it was my own eyes playing tricks on me, but evidently Mr Scrooge had seen something very similar, for the old man tugged hard upon my sleeve and said: 'Ambrose! Ambrose! Do you see it? At the back of Mr Halvorsen?'" "Yes, sir," I said. "I believe I do." "And what was it?" asked the lady. "What did the two of you see?" The train surged by stark countryside outside, the city now but a memory, though Mrs Fell did not notice the landscape. "Captain?" she asked, for the man now seemed far away, lost in thought and memory. Hazily, Ambrose returned his attention to her. "It looked at first like a shadow," he said. "Deep and dense. I took it to be Halvorsen's own. But then it seemed somehow to increase in size, and then almost to wriggle free." "Was there anybody else who saw this extraordinary sight?" Captain Ambrose shook his head. "No, ma'am," he said. "For the little street we were on was by then abandoned. Only Mr Scrooge and I saw what took place – the transformation of shadow into a small squat man, dressed in rags, exactly as Halvorsen had described to us that morning. The appearance was sudden but somehow not shocking, as some natural process had taken place in the blink of an eye." "What did you do?" asked Mrs Fell. "Of course, my instinct was to rush forward and confront the apparition. But Scrooge, he held tightly to my sleeve and ordered me to stand still. 'Don't interrupt,' he hissed. 'We need to bear witness to it all.' And so we stood and watched as the little man in Halvorsen's wake followed, his hands outstretched in a motion that seemed somehow imploring. Mr Scrooge seemed to scrutinise the fellow very carefully. He squinted, took a breath, and then said in a tone of quiet triumph: 'Aha!' "At this, although we were surely too far away for anyone to have heard, the short man turned to look at us. He was far away, but I could still make out the face of what had to be a spirit. The expression that we saw then upon that face will haunt me till the end of my days." "Why? What did it look like?" "It was an expression, ma'am, of absolute malignancy and fury. It was a look of pure hatred and it was directed, without question, at us."

## 一句话总结

第二十八章（Stave Three X）："I suppose," said Captain Ambrose, reaching the crescendo of his tale, "that we had meant to set a kind of trap for the spectre that was pursuing poor Halvorsen. We gave the man, as I have said, an excellent luncheon and bade him rest awhile beneath our roof. The day stayed very hot and the man was quite happy to sit, rather listlessly, in the library and leaf through a volume or two of seafaring tales which Mr Scrooge had produced from some obscure shelf or other. It was only much later, after more food and a little wine, and once dusk had begun to fall, that we said our goodbyes at the entrance to the Club and watched Mr Halvorsen leave and begin his walk through to the city to where he would be staying that night, in an establishment on Goodge Street. "All of these farewells were done with some theatricality, as though we meant to fool some eavesdropper or spectator. The fellow departed, seeming happy enough, and we waited awhile on the threshold, enjoying the relative cool of the evening after the baking heat of the day. Then – as we had agreed some hours before – Mr Scrooge and I began to follow Mr Halvorsen, stepping discreetly behind him, keeping the fellow always in view but also maintaining a respectable distance." "You meant, I suppose," said Mrs Fell, "to see for yourselves if this apparition would take shape behind Mr Halvorsen? Or else to make certain that the ghost wasn't just a real person after all, who would traimpse behind the sailor all night long?" "Exactly right," said Captain Ambrose. "Or – quite frankly, madam – to see if there was anything there at all. Or if the whole thing wasn't entirely in the fellow's head." "So what happened?" "Well, for a long time, at least for an hour or so, we had begun to believe that this was by far the likeliest explanation. There was no sign of any spirit nor of any curious living man. For Halvorsen walked through the streets entirely unaccosted. He took the route that you yourself no doubt took yesterday when you visited the Club, walking up through Piccadilly Circus and then to the streets beyond, moving up towards the Tottenham Court Road. In all this time, the only people that were following Mr Halvorsen were Mr Scrooge and myself. We were about halfway along the length of Tottenham Court Road, at which point Mr Halvorsen had turned right, down a smaller, narrower street, rather poorly lit and with fewer pedestrians, when something strange began to happen. At first, I thought it was my own eyes playing tricks on me, but evidently Mr Scrooge had seen something very similar, for the old man tugged hard upon my sleeve and said: 'Ambrose! Ambrose! Do you see it? At the back of Mr Halvorsen?'" "Yes, sir," I said. "I believe I do." "And what was it?" asked the lady. "What did the two of you see?" The train surged by stark countryside outside, the city now but a memory, though Mrs Fell did not notice the landscape. "Captain?" she asked, for the man now seemed far away, lost in thought and memory. Hazily, Ambrose returned his attention to her. "It looked at first like a shadow," he said. "Deep and dense. I took it to be Halvorsen's own. But then it seemed somehow to increase in size, and then almost to wriggle free." "Was there anybody else who saw this extraordinary sight?" Captain Ambrose shook his head. "No, ma'am," he said. "For the little street we were on was by then abandoned. Only Mr Scrooge and I saw what took place – the transformation of shadow into a small squat man, dressed in rags, exactly as Halvorsen had described to us that morning. The appearance was sudden but somehow not shocking, as some natural process had taken place in the blink of an eye." "What did you do?" asked Mrs Fell. "Of course, my instinct was to rush forward and confront the apparition. But Scrooge, he held tightly to my sleeve and ordered me to stand still. 'Don't interrupt,' he hissed. 'We need to bear witness to it all.' And so we stood and watched as the little man in Halvorsen's wake followed, his hands outstretched in a motion that seemed somehow imploring. Mr Scrooge seemed to scrutinise the fellow very carefully. He squinted, took a breath, and then said in a tone of quiet triumph: 'Aha!'
