# 41. IX

## 本章导航

**一句话概括**：At two o'clock in the morning on Wednesday, eighth of December, Captain Ambrose was woken by the sound of a tremendous hammering upon the front door of the house on Brightwell Road. This was not the single, almost sardonic tap that he had heard the previous evening when he had stood alone in the hallway, but rather a ferocious banging and clattering. It was the kind of noise that one would make if you were trying your utmost to wake a pair of slumbering residents and becoming impatient with their failure to appear. Somewhat to his surprise, Ambrose saw that the candle beside his bed was still lit (how tired and distracted must he have been, he thought, to have neglected to extinguish it upon rolling over for sleep) and so he rose, took the candle and went out to the landing. Here, to his surprise but also to his considerable relief, he found Mrs Fell, who also held her candle out before her. Both were dressed in their nightgowns. Both were bleary-eyed and unsettled. "So you heard it too?" said the lady. "I was beginning to wonder if it was all in my head." As she spoke, another ferocious knocking came at the front door, one floor beneath them. "It's most certainly not, ma'am," said Ambrose. "Someone is out there. And I wish to see who it is. You stay here now." He walked away, candle aloft. He had reached only the top of the staircase when he heard the lady padding indignantly after him. "Certainly not," she said. "I won't be staying anywhere." Down the stairs they nervously trod. There were no further sounds, save for their own ragged breathing. Once they had reached the ground, and were stepping towards the door, there came again three further knocks, but quieter this time, as though whoever it was somehow knew that the captain and the lady were awake now and standing close by. Or perhaps as if they were growing tired. The captain stopped beside the door. "Who's there?" he called out. "Who knocks at our door at such an hour as this?" "Captain!" said the lady. "The rules." "Forget the rules for a moment," said Ambrose. "I want to see exactly who this is." A voice came from the other side of the door then, sounding faint and frail but highly recognisable all the same. "Open up, Captain Ambrose! I've been waiting out here for far too long." That high, rather querulous tone was unmistakeable. "Ebenezer?" said the captain. The voice came back. "Of course it's Ebenezer! Now, in the name of mercy, please open this door." Without thinking, the captain reached for the key on its hook beside the door. The lady grabbed at his arm. "Captain," she hissed. "The first rule!" "I would know that old man's voice anywhere," he said. "Now, we really cannot leave him standing out there in the cold a moment longer. The poor man must be catching his death." "I don't…" the lady began, then broke off as a further double knock came at the door. "Captain, I don't think you should." Ambrose did not reply but put the key into the lock and turned it. "It will all be better," he said, "now that Ebenezer's here." He unlocked the door. The lady shrank back. Ambrose pulled the door back to reveal, standing upon the threshold, none other than Ebenezer Scrooge. He was shivering in the cold and he looked profoundly irritated to have been kept waiting for so long. There was no doubt at all in the mind of Captain Ambrose that it was his old friend and mentor, no doubt at all. At least at first. "Mr Scrooge!" This was Mrs Fell now, her doubts temporarily cast aside. "It's really you! Oh, thank goodness!" "Ebenezer!" Ambrose said. "Whatever has taken you so long?" The warmth in both their voices soon ebbed away, however, as the thing on the doorstep said nothing at all in reply. The old man simply stood there, staring at them both, like a figure in a painting come to life. "Ebenezer?" said Ambrose again, more carefully this time. The old man did not speak but only began to smile. Not a kind smile nor one of happy reunion but an awful sneering kind of grin, a peeling back of his lips to show the withered, yellowing teeth beyond. In a darkness that was broken only by the two flickering candles, Mrs Fell stole close to the captain's side. "Ebenezer, please," Ambrose said, though he had by then, of course, come to understand that whatever stood before him, it was no friend of his. With that hideous grin growing still wider and more horrible, the figure of Ebenezer Scrooge began to slump forward and to crouch, his posture becoming similar to that of a monkey. There was something mocking and dreadful in the sight. It (for one can hardly continue to refer to it as 'he' a moment longer) seemed to be making ready to spring. Still no sound of any kind came from it, a fact which somehow made the whole performance still more awful and dream-like. It crouched low upon the ground, like an animal squatting upon its hind legs, a pose that would have been quite impossible for that elderly gentleman whose form this entity had adopted. At last, Mrs Fell screamed. The thing looked as though it was getting ready to spring towards them. "Close the door! Close the door!" the lady shouted. But it was too late. The thing that wore the form of Ebenezer Scrooge sprang towards them both, moving through the air with a speed that was quite impossible. Both Ambrose and Mrs Fell stumbled backwards, acting on pure instinct, but the thing somehow passed straight through them, like it was made of so much mist. As this occurred, there came a sound as of rushing wind, and the lady and the gentlemen fell together upon the ground. Their candles were extinguished. They rose uncertainly to their feet. Of the being – the gaseous being? – there was now no sign at all. Though there was a smell, most unpleasant, which lingered for a few moments before dissipating. Mrs Fell looked at the captain, and he looked at her. "I feel," she began, speaking as steadily as she was able, "I feel as if I've been the victim of some horrible joke…" The captain did not reply but only held her tightly. She permitted this for a moment, then stepped away from him. "If it is a joke," he said, "then it's not a very funny one." He stepped outside, looked up and down the darkened street. Seeing no one, he came back in and closed the door again. The smell was dissipating. "We can beat this," he said firmly. "Whatever this thing is in here, we can beat it." "Are you sure of that?" asked the lady, realising too late that she had formed a question. From somewhere up above them, there came a single, laconic knock. She looked again at the captain and he at her. "Come," he said, "let's see if we can stoke up the fire a bit for a while." And so they went to the drawing room and sat by the fire, which was slumbering but not extinct, and they found a little warmth there. They sat together, closer than convention would allow, and each said nothing at all to the other. There was between them a kind of unspoken agreement that this was not the place or the time to discuss what they had seen or to speak aloud the worst of their suspicions. In spite of what they had witnessed, and in spite of the strong sensation (they both felt it) that the house seemed somehow to be watching them with mocking eyes, in the end they fell asleep together before the fire, the lady's head resting delicately upon the gentleman's shoulder. Fear brings with it its own kind of exhaustion. And so, in this manner, was that long night passed.

## 一句话总结

第四十一章（Stave Three IX）：At two o'clock in the morning on Wednesday, eighth of December, Captain Ambrose was woken by the sound of a tremendous hammering upon the front door of the house on Brightwell Road. This was not the single, almost sardonic tap that he had heard the previous evening when he had stood alone in the hallway, but rather a ferocious banging and clattering. It was the kind of noise that one would make if you were trying your utmost to wake a pair of slumbering residents and becoming impatient with their failure to appear. Somewhat to his surprise, Ambrose saw that the candle beside his bed was still lit (how tired and distracted must he have been, he thought, to have neglected to extinguish it upon rolling over for sleep) and so he rose, took the candle and went out to the landing. Here, to his surprise but also to his considerable relief, he found Mrs Fell, who also held her candle out before her. Both were dressed in their nightgowns. Both were bleary-eyed and unsettled. "So you heard it too?" said the lady. "I was beginning to wonder if it was all in my head." As she spoke, another ferocious knocking came at the front door, one floor beneath them. "It's most certainly not, ma'am," said Ambrose. "Someone is out there. And I wish to see who it is. You stay here now." He walked away, candle aloft. He had reached only the top of the staircase when he heard the lady padding indignantly after him. "Certainly not," she said. "I won't be staying anywhere." Down the stairs they nervously trod. There were no further sounds, save for their own ragged breathing. Once they had reached the ground, and were stepping towards the door, there came again three further knocks, but quieter this time, as though whoever it was somehow knew that the captain and the lady were awake now and standing close by. Or perhaps as if they were growing tired. The captain stopped beside the door. "Who's there?" he called out. "Who knocks at our door at such an hour as this?" "Captain!" said the lady. "The rules." "Forget the rules for a moment," said Ambrose. "I want to see exactly who this is." A voice came from the other side of the door then, sounding faint and frail but highly recognisable all the same. "Open up, Captain Ambrose! I've been waiting out here for far too long." That high, rather querulous tone was unmistakeable. "Ebenezer?" said the captain. The voice came back. "Of course it's Ebenezer! Now, in the name of mercy, please open this door." Without thinking, the captain reached for the key on its hook beside the door. The lady grabbed at his arm. "Captain," she hissed. "The first rule!" "I would know that old man's voice anywhere," he said. "Now, we really cannot leave him standing out there in the cold a moment longer. The poor man must be catching his death." "I don't…" the lady began, then broke off as a further double knock came at the door. "Captain, I don't think you should." Ambrose did not reply but put the key into the lock and turned it. "It will all be better," he said, "now that Ebenezer's here." He unlocked the door. The lady shrank back. Ambrose pulled the door back to reveal, standing upon the threshold, none other than Ebenezer Scrooge. He was shivering in the cold and he looked profoundly irritated to have been kept waiting for so long. There was no doubt at all in the mind of Captain Ambrose that it was his old friend and mentor, no doubt at all. At least at first. "Mr Scrooge!" This was Mrs Fell now, her doubts temporarily cast aside. "It's really you! Oh, thank goodness!" "Ebenezer!" Ambrose said. "Whatever has taken you so long?" The warmth in both their voices soon ebbed away, however, as the thing on the doorstep said nothing at all in reply. The old man simply stood there, staring at them both, like a figure in a painting come to life. "Ebenezer?" said Ambrose again, more carefully this time. The old man did not speak but only began to smile. Not a kind smile nor one of happy reunion but an awful sneering kind of grin, a peeling back of his lips to show the withered, yellowing teeth beyond. In a darkness that was broken only by the two flickering candles, Mrs Fell stole close to the captain's side. "Ebenezer, please," Ambrose said, though he had by then, of course, come to understand that whatever stood before him, it was no friend of his. With that hideous grin growing still wider and more horrible, the figure of Ebenezer Scrooge began to slump forward and to crouch, his posture becoming similar to that of a monkey. There was something mocking and dreadful in the sight. It (for one can hardly continue to refer to it as 'he' a moment longer) seemed to be making ready to spring. Still no sound of any kind came from it, a fact which somehow made the whole performance still more awful and dream-like. It crouched low upon the ground, like an animal squatting upon its hind legs, a pose that would have been quite impossible for that elderly gentleman whose form this entity had adopted. At last, Mrs Fell screamed. The thing looked as though it was getting ready to spring towards them. "Close the door! Close the door!" the lady shouted. But it was too late. The thing that wore the form of Ebenezer Scrooge sprang towards them both, moving through the air with a speed that was quite impossible. Both Ambrose and Mrs Fell stumbled backwards, acting on pure instinct, but the thing somehow passed straight through them, like it was made of so much mist. As this occurred, there came a sound as of rushing wind, and the lady and the gentlemen fell together upon the ground. Their candles were extinguished. They rose uncertainly to their feet. Of the being – the gaseous being? – there was now no sign at all. Though there was a smell, most unpleasant, which lingered for a few moments before dissipating. Mrs Fell looked at the captain, and he looked at her. "I feel," she began, speaking as steadily as she was able, "I feel as if I've been the victim of some horrible joke…" The captain did not reply but only held her tightly. She permitted this for a moment, then stepped away from him. "If it is a joke," he said, "then it's not a very funny one." He stepped outside, looked up and down the darkened street. Seeing no one, he came back in and closed the door again. The smell was dissipating. "We can beat this," he said firmly. "Whatever this thing is in here, we can beat it." "Are you sure of that?" asked the lady, realising too late that she had formed a question. From somewhere up above them, there came a single, laconic knock. She looked again at the captain and he at her. "Come," he said, "let's see if we can stoke up the fire a bit for a while." And so they went to the drawing room and sat by the fire, which was slumbering but not extinct, and they found a little warmth there. They sat together, closer than convention would allow, and each said nothing at all to the other. There was between them a kind of unspoken agreement that this was not the place or the time to discuss what they had seen or to speak aloud the worst of their suspicions. In spite of what they had witnessed, and in spite of the strong sensation (they both felt it) that the house seemed somehow to be watching them with mocking eyes, in the end they fell asleep together before the fire, the lady's head resting delicately upon the gentleman's shoulder. Fear brings with it its own kind of exhaustion.
