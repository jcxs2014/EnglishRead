# 26. VIII

## 本章导航

**一句话概括**：Emilia Hare – the young lady who worked most days and nights behind the public bar at The Jolly Boaters – had grown to hate her life, and everyone in it, with a passion. She hated her mother (whose idea it had been that she take a job in that noisy and malodorous establishment in the first place) and she hated her father (who had said nothing but only acquiesced, as ever, to his commanding wife). She hated her employer (that foul-mouthed gentleman called Bill Crickmore who resorted too often to the contents of his own cellar), she hated the clientele of the public house (a mixture of intoxicated lawyers who drank too quickly, and shifty-eyed passers-by who idled the day away with a single pint of porter). She hated the old men who leered and tried to paw at her, and she hated men of her own age who looked at her like sheep and could barely muster up a single sentence. She wore at all times in public a look of imperishable scorn which, to her infuriation, seemed to serve only to attract admirers to a greater degree, like wasps to buttered toast on a summer's afternoon. There was for Miss Hare but one exception to this pantheon of irritants, just one person who coaxed from her lips a real smile and whose conversation inspired in her something like affection, and it was this gentleman who walked into the public bar at a little after noon and stumbled towards her. He was a tall man, Miss Hare's friend – tall, dark-haired, slim and, in the ordinary run of things, decidedly good-looking. He had a gentle way about him and a rare ability both for perception and the taking of interest in his fellow man – although it must be said none of this was visible in him that afternoon. He seemed to be swaying on his feet as he moved. He looked ragged, unkempt, as though he had barely slept (or not slept at all) the preceding night, and if he had done so, then it was in the clothes that he still wore now. He saw her, made his way towards her and called out her name. "Miss Hare!" The woman in question looked a little nervously about him. For it was evident that her particular friend had been drinking already and her employer, Crickmore, was fond of nothing more than slinging out any inebriate to whom he had taken a dislike. Thankfully, the landlord was deep in conversation in a snug with some bloated old acquaintance and he had not yet seen the arrival. "Archie!" said Miss Hare as softly as she could, at which the man came closer and leant against the bar, much in the manner of a drowning mariner grasping for driftwood. "Whatever is the matter?" He was close enough to her now that she could see the bloodshot whites of his eyes. Of his usual kindly, thoughtful, quietly urbane self, there was nothing to be seen at all. Instead, he seemed to be a man on the very edge of some catastrophe, like one who has awoken from a nightmare to find the world around him in a state identical to his dream. "I think…" began Mr Archie Beck, slurring his words in spite of his best efforts, "I think that I have opened a door which I may not be able to close again." "Tell me," said Miss Hare, her heart beating with an excitement she hadn't known for many months, "tell me everything." "Oh, but there's a voice," he said. "A voice in my head which won't let me rest. Won't let me do anything but what he wishes. A man, a dead man, called Marley." Miss Hare looked at him with great pity, and just a little excitement as well. "Quick," she said, checking once again that the landlord remained distracted. "Take this. Let's hope it calms you down." The most celebrated young psychic medium in London seized the glass gratefully and drank deep of the dark and swirling liquid. "This is not the first drink that I have taken today," he said solemnly. This the lady did not find surprising. "But it seems…" he said. "You are right. It does seem to quieten him down. Nothing else has any effect on him. Even the salt didn't keep him out for long." "What's happened?" the young woman asked, reaching out her right hand across the bar, almost within touching distance of Mr Beck's fingertips. "You can tell me anything, you know. Often better to share than to keep things to yourself." "I wish I could," he said. "But it wouldn't be right. It wouldn't be fair." He took another swig of his drink and then seemed suddenly very far away, as though he heard some other voice which she could not make out. Then Mr Crickmore bustled over and insisted she help him with a barrel, and so, with great regret, she had to be drawn away from her conversation. Miss Hare looked back once more at her friend, who sat upright at the bar, his glass of porter now almost empty before him, and wearing an expression of great anxiety upon his face, like that of a man under some unendurable strain who might, at any moment, break. When she returned from the cellar, having watched a perspiring Mr Crickmore change the barrel, it was to find the bar empty and Mr Beck gone. At the sight of the empty chair where he had sat just minutes before she felt an anxious sadness, as though she somehow knew the dreadful act which he was about to commit.

## 一句话总结

第二十六章（Stave Three VIII）：Emilia Hare – the young lady who worked most days and nights behind the public bar at The Jolly Boaters – had grown to hate her life, and everyone in it, with a passion. She hated her mother (whose idea it had been that she take a job in that noisy and malodorous establishment in the first place) and she hated her father (who had said nothing but only acquiesced, as ever, to his commanding wife). She hated her employer (that foul-mouthed gentleman called Bill Crickmore who resorted too often to the contents of his own cellar), she hated the clientele of the public house (a mixture of intoxicated lawyers who drank too quickly, and shifty-eyed passers-by who idled the day away with a single pint of porter). She hated the old men who leered and tried to paw at her, and she hated men of her own age who looked at her like sheep and could barely muster up a single sentence. She wore at all times in public a look of imperishable scorn which, to her infuriation, seemed to serve only to attract admirers to a greater degree, like wasps to buttered toast on a summer's afternoon. There was for Miss Hare but one exception to this pantheon of irritants, just one person who coaxed from her lips a real smile and whose conversation inspired in her something like affection, and it was this gentleman who walked into the public bar at a little after noon and stumbled towards her. He was a tall man, Miss Hare's friend – tall, dark-haired, slim and, in the ordinary run of things, decidedly good-looking. He had a gentle way about him and a rare ability both for perception and the taking of interest in his fellow man – although it must be said none of this was visible in him that afternoon. He seemed to be swaying on his feet as he moved. He looked ragged, unkempt, as though he had barely slept (or not slept at all) the preceding night, and if he had done so, then it was in the clothes that he still wore now. He saw her, made his way towards her and called out her name. "Miss Hare!" The woman in question looked a little nervously about him. For it was evident that her particular friend had been drinking already and her employer, Crickmore, was fond of nothing more than slinging out any inebriate to whom he had taken a dislike. Thankfully, the landlord was deep in conversation in a snug with some bloated old acquaintance and he had not yet seen the arrival. "Archie!" said Miss Hare as softly as she could, at which the man came closer and leant against the bar, much in the manner of a drowning mariner grasping for driftwood. "Whatever is the matter?" He was close enough to her now that she could see the bloodshot whites of his eyes. Of his usual kindly, thoughtful, quietly urbane self, there was nothing to be seen at all. Instead, he seemed to be a man on the very edge of some catastrophe, like one who has awoken from a nightmare to find the world around him in a state identical to his dream. "I think…" began Mr Archie Beck, slurring his words in spite of his best efforts, "I think that I have opened a door which I may not be able to close again." "Tell me," said Miss Hare, her heart beating with an excitement she hadn't known for many months, "tell me everything." "Oh, but there's a voice," he said. "A voice in my head which won't let me rest. Won't let me do anything but what he wishes. A man, a dead man, called Marley." Miss Hare looked at him with great pity, and just a little excitement as well. "Quick," she said, checking once again that the landlord remained distracted. "Take this. Let's hope it calms you down." The most celebrated young psychic medium in London seized the glass gratefully and drank deep of the dark and swirling liquid. "This is not the first drink that I have taken today," he said solemnly. This the lady did not find surprising. "But it seems…" he said. "You are right. It does seem to quieten him down. Nothing else has any effect on him. Even the salt didn't keep him out for long." "What's happened?" the young woman asked, reaching out her right hand across the bar, almost within touching distance of Mr Beck's fingertips. "You can tell me anything, you know. Often better to share than to keep things to yourself." "I wish I could," he said. "But it wouldn't be right. It wouldn't be fair." He took another swig of his drink and then seemed suddenly very far away, as though he heard some other voice which she could not make out. Then Mr Crickmore bustled over and insisted she help him with a barrel, and so, with great regret, she had to be drawn away from her conversation. Miss Hare looked back once more at her friend, who sat upright at the bar, his glass of porter now almost empty before him, and wearing an expression of great anxiety upon his face, like that of a man under some unendurable strain who might, at any moment, break. When she returned from the cellar, having watched a perspiring Mr Crickmore change the barrel, it was to find the bar empty and Mr Beck gone.
