# 30. XII

## 本章导航

**一句话概括**：When Mr Scrooge returned to the Club it was to pack, as briskly as he could, a modest travelling bag with those few essentials as he required for a stay away from home. This accomplished, he visited the secretary of the Club, whose offices were on the first floor, to inform him of his intended absence from his quarters for several nights to come. The secretary – a wispy-haired gentleman of sixty-five whose name was Bentinck – looked up from some sheaf of papers (in the annotation of which he had been engaged) to say how sorry he was to hear of Mr Scrooge’s forthcoming absence. “You’ll be missed, sir, have no doubt of that. You bring a decided character to the place. Yes, sir, you do. There’s no denying it.” Scrooge thanked Mr Bentinck for his kind words. “And should anyone come visiting, sir… in answer to your advertisement… what are we to say to them?” “Only that I am away and that I may be away for some time, and that if I return, then I shall be only too glad to offer them my assistance on my return.” “If, sir?” said Mr Bentinck. “If you return?” Scrooge paled. “I meant to say when I return, of course.” “But you didn’t, sir. No. You said: if.” “Well then, you must forgive me, Mr Bentinck. It’s age, sir, that’s all it is.” Bentinck shook his head. “I do hope so, Mr Scrooge, I surely do. For we’d be in an awful pickle without you, sir. Truly.” “I’m rather touched, Mr Bentinck, to hear you speak so.” The secretary, who now felt he’d gone rather too far, merely grunted and returned to his papers. Scrooge bade farewell and left the man to his labours. He called for the maid, Charlotte, and said his farewells to her too. She promised to keep his rooms spick and span, to which he said that he had no doubt that she would. He gave her a sovereign, as was his wont, for she was an honest and hardworking girl who was well deserving of such largesse. Then Mr Scrooge, without thinking, went downstairs and let himself out of the Club. He stepped out once again into the crisp air of the day and meant to walk towards the main thoroughfare and there hail a cab to the station. He was not to get so far as this. For standing before him, a few feet back from the threshold, was a man he recognised at once as— “Mr Beck?” The psychic medium looked altered indeed from their last encounter on the previous night. His eyes were bloodshot. He was trembling. In spite of the cold of the day, his face was slick with perspiration. Scrooge felt, in the first instance, a great swell of pity at the sight of him. “What’s happened to you, Mr Beck? Forgive me, but you don’t look at all well.” Beck did not reply at first but rather drew out a pistol, which he pointed, with an awful kind of nonchalance, in the direction of Mr Scrooge. “There is a voice, sir,” said the psychic. “A voice in my head that says he knows you. He says his name is Marley.” “You said as much last night,” said Scrooge. “I was not certain of it then, and I am still not certain of it now.” Beck stepped closer. Scrooge did not flinch. “He keeps talking to me,” said Beck. “He won’t let me be until this is done.” Scrooge did his best to gather himself to his full height. “Until what is done, sir?” “You’re not to go to Starningham, Mr Scrooge. You’re not to go to Brightwell Road.” The old man peered at the spiritualist. He saw that the pistol was primed and ready. He said: “And why is that exactly, eh? Why can’t I go there?” At the asking of this question, something very peculiar took place. A shadow seemed to pass across the psychic. His pupils grew very wide. And when he spoke again his voice was subtly different – older, cracked – and it was as though someone entirely different had borrowed, temporarily, his lips and larynx. “Do not go,” said whatever it was that had so tormented Mr Beck. “Do not go to Starningham. Leave that woman be.” Scrooge had seen many fraudulent mediums in his time, but Beck, assuredly, was not one of them. At least not upon this occasion. “Who are you?” Scrooge said softly. “You. Know. Who. I. Am.” “No, sir, indeed I do not. For whoever you are, you’re not Jacob Marley. You’re somebody else entirely. And so I should like to know two things – precisely who you are and precisely why you are lying to me.” The pupils of Mr Beck grew darker and larger still. His face seemed to retreat into shadow. There was to his movements, then, an awful sense of puppetry (which lends considerable credence, I would say, to his subsequent claim to be able to remember nothing at all after his arrival at the door to the Club). The pistol he brought up to face Mr Scrooge’s chest. Ebenezer, for his part, felt at this a great and somehow surprising sense of peace. There was no time for him to turn or run in any case, only for a little carousel of memory to trip swiftly through his head – Belle, Mr Fezziwig, the Spirits, the Cratchits, Captain Ambrose and Mrs Fell – before fire burst forth from the barrel of the pistol and a corresponding pain hit him hard in the chest. He crumpled to his knees and fell slowly onto his back. The psychic was by then returned to himself. He looked around him in anguish. He dropped the pistol. He turned and fled, desperate, into the city. Behind him came the sounds of a woman screaming. Charlotte, having heard the commotion, had emerged onto the doorstep. She was already calling for help, cradling the old man in her arms. He was close enough for her to hear his wheezing, ragged, struggling breaths. Scrooge struggled on, but all around him the world was draining, at speed, of light and life and colour. Instead, the darkness – pitch black now and hungry – surged up on every side of him, dragging him down and down in the void, until Ebenezer Scrooge was lost utterly in the enormity of it.

## 一句话总结

第三十章（Stave Three XII）：When Mr Scrooge returned to the Club it was to pack, as briskly as he could, a modest travelling bag with those few essentials as he required for a stay away from home. This accomplished, he visited the secretary of the Club, whose offices were on the first floor, to inform him of his intended absence from his quarters for several nights to come. The secretary – a wispy-haired gentleman of sixty-five whose name was Bentinck – looked up from some sheaf of papers (in the annotation of which he had been engaged) to say how sorry he was to hear of Mr Scrooge’s forthcoming absence. “You’ll be missed, sir, have no doubt of that. You bring a decided character to the place. Yes, sir, you do. There’s no denying it.” Scrooge thanked Mr Bentinck for his kind words. “And should anyone come visiting, sir… in answer to your advertisement… what are we to say to them?” “Only that I am away and that I may be away for some time, and that if I return, then I shall be only too glad to offer them my assistance on my return.” “If, sir?” said Mr Bentinck. “If you return?” Scrooge paled. “I meant to say when I return, of course.” “But you didn’t, sir. No. You said: if.” “Well then, you must forgive me, Mr Bentinck. It’s age, sir, that’s all it is.” Bentinck shook his head. “I do hope so, Mr Scrooge, I surely do. For we’d be in an awful pickle without you, sir. Truly.” “I’m rather touched, Mr Bentinck, to hear you speak so.” The secretary, who now felt he’d gone rather too far, merely grunted and returned to his papers. Scrooge bade farewell and left the man to his labours. He called for the maid, Charlotte, and said his farewells to her too. She promised to keep his rooms spick and span, to which he said that he had no doubt that she would. He gave her a sovereign, as was his wont, for she was an honest and hardworking girl who was well deserving of such largesse. Then Mr Scrooge, without thinking, went downstairs and let himself out of the Club. He stepped out once again into the crisp air of the day and meant to walk towards the main thoroughfare and there hail a cab to the station. He was not to get so far as this. For standing before him, a few feet back from the threshold, was a man he recognised at once as— “Mr Beck?” The psychic medium looked altered indeed from their last encounter on the previous night. His eyes were bloodshot. He was trembling. In spite of the cold of the day, his face was slick with perspiration. Scrooge felt, in the first instance, a great swell of pity at the sight of him. “What’s happened to you, Mr Beck? Forgive me, but you don’t look at all well.” Beck did not reply at first but rather drew out a pistol, which he pointed, with an awful kind of nonchalance, in the direction of Mr Scrooge. “There is a voice, sir,” said the psychic. “A voice in my head that says he knows you. He says his name is Marley.” “You said as much last night,” said Scrooge. “I was not certain of it then, and I am still not certain of it now.” Beck stepped closer. Scrooge did not flinch. “He keeps talking to me,” said Beck. “He won’t let me be until this is done.” Scrooge did his best to gather himself to his full height. “Until what is done, sir?” “You’re not to go to Starningham, Mr Scrooge. You’re not to go to Brightwell Road.” The old man peered at the spiritualist. He saw that the pistol was primed and ready. He said: “And why is that exactly, eh? Why can’t I go there?” At the asking of this question, something very peculiar took place. A shadow seemed to pass across the psychic. His pupils grew very wide. And when he spoke again his voice was subtly different – older, cracked – and it was as though someone entirely different had borrowed, temporarily, his lips and larynx. “Do not go,” said whatever it was that had so tormented Mr Beck. “Do not go to Starningham. Leave that woman be.” Scrooge had seen many fraudulent mediums in his time, but Beck, assuredly, was not one of them. At least not upon this occasion. “Who are you?” Scrooge said softly. “You. Know. Who. I. Am.” “No, sir, indeed I do not. For whoever you are, you’re not Jacob Marley. You’re somebody else entirely. And so I should like to know two things – precisely who you are and precisely why you are lying to me.” The pupils of Mr Beck grew darker and larger still. His face seemed to retreat into shadow. There was to his movements, then, an awful sense of puppetry (which lends considerable credence, I would say, to his subsequent claim to be able to remember nothing at all after his arrival at the door to the Club). The pistol he brought up to face Mr Scrooge’s chest. Ebenezer, for his part, felt at this a great and somehow surprising sense of peace. There was no time for him to turn or run in any case, only for a little carousel of memory to trip swiftly through his head – Belle, Mr Fezziwig, the Spirits, the Cratchits, Captain Ambrose and Mrs Fell – before fire burst forth from the barrel of the pistol and a corresponding pain hit him hard in the chest. He crumpled to his knees and fell slowly onto his back. The psychic was by then returned to himself. He looked around him in anguish. He dropped the pistol. He turned and fled, desperate, into the city. Behind him came the sounds of a woman screaming. Charlotte, having heard the commotion, had emerged onto the doorstep. She was already calling for help, cradling the old man in her arms. He was close enough for her to hear his wheezing, ragged, struggling breaths. Scrooge struggled on, but all around him the world was draining, at speed, of light and life and colour.
