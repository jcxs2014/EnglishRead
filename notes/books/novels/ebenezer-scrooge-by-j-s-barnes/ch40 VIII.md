# 40. VIII

## 本章导航

**一句话概括**：I don't believe that Mr Scrooge can be joining us tonight after all." This was said by Mrs Fell and it was said with a kind of forced lightness, as though the matter of which she spoke was somehow of very little moment. "I fear you may be right," replied the captain, in an approximation of the same, dishonest tone. "Perhaps something has delayed him," suggested the lady. "Oh no doubt. No doubt. We can only hope it's nothing serious. I'm sure he will be here tomorrow. Perhaps even in the morning." They were sitting in the dining room together with the remnants of their supper spread out on the table before them. The scene looked like one of happy domesticity. "I don't believe," said the lady thoughtfully, "that I expected so warm a welcome somehow. Or for things to have been made so comfortable for us." The captain remarked that he shared her surprise. "It's just all so very ordinary. Pleasant, yes, but ordinary too." "But Mrs Bethlem's rules…" Captain Ambrose began. Supper and the time that had passed since the departure of that lady seemed to have softened Mrs Fell in her opinion of it all. "I do think we should follow them," she admitted. "Yet she has been here a very long time, in this little place, and it would hardly be unusual if all manner of queer beliefs and superstitions should spring up in her." "These things are quite likely," said the captain. "But I should like to know…" He paused then, to make sure that he did not phrase what followed as a question. "I should like to know what it was that you discussed with Mrs Bethlem on the steps outside." "I asked her about my uncle," the lady said simply. "For it sounded to me as though she had known him well in life. I asked what he was like, for I was just a child when I met him, and I asked if any of the rumours about him were true." She took a sip from the glass that lay before her. "Her replies were as follows. Firstly, that she wasn't sure if my uncle was the kind of man that anyone ever really knew at all well. She had never met anybody, she said, who might be said to be close to the man. But that she could only speak as she found and that my uncle had been to her a very fair – no, more than fair – a very generous employer and that he had been considerable material help to her and her husband, even after his death. I had the strong sense that there was more that she might have said then. But she seemed so eager to depart that I let her." "So, we're on our own," stated the captain. "Only until Mr Scrooge gets here." "Yes." Without meaning to, Ambrose made the word sound full of doubt. "Only until then." He rose abruptly to his feet. "Now, I shall tidy this away. We have the girl visiting with us first thing tomorrow to keep things nice and shipshape. And it's been a long day. So, please, might I suggest that we go to our beds and see if we can't both of us have a good night's sleep?" He stopped short then, for he realised that he had asked a question aloud. Mrs Fell must have realised the same, for she looked at him with an expression of distinct nervousness upon her face. After that? Nothing. Nothing at all. But only the quietness of the little town. Mrs Fell smiled at the captain, and it was a smile of tentative but growing relief. "Yes," she said. "I quite agree. And then we can see what everything looks like in the daytime." Now, there is no need to trouble you with what followed. With the setting aside of the dinner and the remainder of the food. With Captain Ambrose's careful locking of the front door, with his almost reverential hanging up of the key upon a hook which had been placed in the hallway for that purpose, and with his making sure that the house was as secure as it could possibly be. With their making ready for bed, with all their nervous preparations. They said good night to one another upon the landing, standing a goodly distance apart. "I do hope that you sleep well, ma'am," said the captain. "And I wish the same for you," said the lady. "Thank you for all that you have done today. For, in spite of it all, I should not have wished to have come here alone." "You're very welcome, ma'am. Very welcome indeed." They exchanged a small smile. Then they said good night once again and they went to their separate rooms. Both bore with them a candle. Both looked rather nervously about their chambers, but both also discovered that there was nothing at all to be worried about, even as the candlelight threw capering shadows upon the walls. Both discovered that the beds which Mrs Bethlem had made up for them were comfortable indeed. And, to their surprise, both of them found themselves drifting off very easily and lightly to sleep. By now it was around ten o'clock at night. And for the following four hours Captain Ambrose and Mrs Louisa Fell slept well and deeply in their beds. This was to be the last time that they would do so in that house.

第四十章（Stave Three VIII）：I don't believe that Mr Scrooge can be joining us tonight after all." This was said by Mrs Fell and it was said with a kind of forced lightness, as though the matter of which she spoke was somehow of very little moment. "I fear you may be right," replied the captain, in an approximation of the same, dishonest tone. "Perhaps something has delayed him," suggested the lady. "Oh no doubt. No doubt. We can only hope it's nothing serious. I'm sure he will be here tomorrow. Perhaps even in the morning." They were sitting in the dining room together with the remnants of their supper spread out on the table before them. The scene looked like one of happy domesticity. "I don't believe," said the lady thoughtfully, "that I expected so warm a welcome somehow. Or for things to have been made so comfortable for us." The captain remarked that he shared her surprise. "It's just all so very ordinary. Pleasant, yes, but ordinary too." "But Mrs Bethlem's rules…" Captain Ambrose began. Supper and the time that had passed since the departure of that lady seemed to have softened Mrs Fell in her opinion of it all. "I do think we should follow them," she admitted. "Yet she has been here a very long time, in this little place, and it would hardly be unusual if all manner of queer beliefs and superstitions should spring up in her." "These things are quite likely," said the captain. "But I should like to know…" He paused then, to make sure that he did not phrase what followed as a question. "I should like to know what it was that you discussed with Mrs Bethlem on the steps outside." "I asked her about my uncle," the lady said simply. "For it sounded to me as though she had known him well in life. I asked what he was like, for I was just a child when I met him, and I asked if any of the rumours about him were true." She took a sip from the glass that lay before her. "Her replies were as follows. Firstly, that she wasn't sure if my uncle was the kind of man that anyone ever really knew at all well. She had never met anybody, she said, who might be said to be close to the man. But that she could only speak as she found and that my uncle had been to her a very fair – no, more than fair – a very generous employer and that he had been considerable material help to her and her husband, even after his death. I had the strong sense that there was more that she might have said then. But she seemed so eager to depart that I let her." "So, we're on our own," stated the captain. "Only until Mr Scrooge gets here." "Yes." Without meaning to, Ambrose made the word sound full of doubt. "Only until then." He rose abruptly to his feet. "Now, I shall tidy this away. We have the girl visiting with us first thing tomorrow to keep things nice and shipshape. And it's been a long day. So, please, might I suggest that we go to our beds and see if we can't both of us have a good night's sleep?" He stopped short then, for he realised that he had asked a question aloud. Mrs Fell must have realised the same, for she looked at him with an expression of distinct nervousness upon her face. After that? Nothing. Nothing at all. But only the quietness of the little town. Mrs Fell smiled at the captain, and it was a smile of tentative but growing relief. "Yes," she said. "I quite agree. And then we can see what everything looks like in the daytime." Now, there is no need to trouble you with what followed. With the setting aside of the dinner and the remainder of the food. With Captain Ambrose's careful locking of the front door, with his almost reverential hanging up of the key upon a hook which had been placed in the hallway for that purpose, and with his making sure that the house was as secure as it could possibly be. With their making ready for bed, with all their nervous preparations. They said good night to one another upon the landing, standing a goodly distance apart. "I do hope that you sleep well, ma'am," said the captain. "And I wish the same for you," said the lady. "Thank you for all that you have done today. For, in spite of it all, I should not have wished to have come here alone." "You're very welcome, ma'am. Very welcome indeed." They exchanged a small smile. Then they said good night once again and they went to their separate rooms. Both bore with them a candle. Both looked rather nervously about their chambers, but both also discovered that there was nothing at all to be worried about, even as the candlelight threw capering shadows upon the walls. Both discovered that the beds which Mrs Bethlem had made up for them were comfortable indeed. And, to their surprise, both of them found themselves drifting off very easily and lightly to sleep.
