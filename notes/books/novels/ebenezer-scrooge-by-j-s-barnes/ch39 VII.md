# 39. VII

## 本章导航

**一句话概括**：In Starningham, the tour of the house on Brightwell Road did not take long. As a guide, Mrs Bethlem was thorough but decidedly brisk. It soon became apparent that the house was really in excellent condition, given its circumstances. In many ways, it was a brighter (and surely a cleaner) place now than it had been when Gowing himself had lived here. The rooms were neatly kept and well furnished. If it had something of the air of a kind of museum, preserved but unchanging, then that was surely only a tribute to the industry and honesty of Mrs Bethlem and her late husband. The first floor had three bedrooms, and it was quickly arranged between the captain and the lady as to who should have each chamber. Theirs would be opposite one another, separated by a small landing. If Mrs Bethlem perceived anything indecorous or inappropriate in such an arrangement, then she did not say so. They were even permitted (for, they both later agreed, that is exactly how it felt) to see the uppermost floor. A candle was lit by Mrs Bethlem and given to the captain. He held it out before him while Mrs Fell stood by his side. The space was not so very alarming, even seen by flickering candlelight alone. It was nothing like so clean and tidy as the two storeys which lay below it, but merely a large, unfurnished room. The only attempts at comfort of any kind were three big, frayed rugs which had been laid out upon the wooden floor. A mirror, ornate and framed with silver, hung upon the wall, as it had all those years ago. The place was quite considerably colder than the rest of the house as well. "So this," said the captain, "must be where you slept when you last visited." "It was." "What a very strange choice… To put a child in here." The lady did not reply but only shook her head and turned to leave. With some relief, the pair descended the staircase to the first floor, where Mrs Bethlem was waiting for them with something like vindication glinting in her eyes. After that, there was little enough left for them to do. Besides, the woman seemed eager to be away. Back on the ground floor, in the hallway, Mrs Fell thanked her again for her labours. "You're very welcome," Mrs Bethlem said. "And I know you will be perfectly happy here. You and your little one. So long as you remember what I told you. As to anything else, I dare say you'll just have to discover it on your own. For this is a house of some surprises. Even your uncle found that to be true." "Mrs Bethlem, if I may," began Mrs Fell, only for the other woman to hold up her hand to stop her speech. "I was quite in earnest earlier," she said. "If you wish to ask a question, then please God let us do it outside these walls. I have a few minutes more. And if I can answer them I shall. But, please, please let us do so outside. After that, I must be away. Don't fret. I shall tell you my address in case you should need me again." Another glance was exchanged between the captain and the lady at this, but, of course, Mrs Fell ended by saying that she quite understood. And so the door was opened and the two women retired to the cold of the street outside. For the first time all day, the captain was left alone. He had for many years been by nature a solitary man and he really ought to have enjoyed a few moments of quiet. Yet, on the contrary, he found it difficult not to throw open the door again and go out into the night. For there was – absurd though it might sound – a strong sense that the house in which he stood was curious about him, and that the brand of curiosity was not a friendly or an innocent one. He found that he rather bristled at the notion. Then, speaking in part from bravado and in part from true irritation after a long and often vexing day, he called out: "So why can't we ask any questions in here, then? What's so wrong about asking b—y questions?" It was, perhaps, a mere coincidence, but it sounded very much at that moment as if a single knock was heard, not from outside the house, at door or window, but from somewhere within it. It did not sound like a creak or like a noise which an old house might make when, having been left empty for so long, it suddenly acquires new residents. No, thought the captain, it sounded quite distinctly like the sound of knuckles upon wood. He waited but no further sound could be heard. "Oh, hurry up, Ebenezer," he said defiantly. "In God's name, hurry up and get to Starningham." Then the door opened again and Mrs Fell returned to the house. She was alone. "Mrs Bethlem's gone home," she said by way of explanation. "And I don't know about you, Captain, but I am now very hungry indeed. I think we should eat. And I think also that we should follow Mrs Bethlem's rules." "I think the same," was all that the captain said to this, "and on both counts too." They retired together to the kitchen then, to see what Mrs Bethlem had left them for their supper, in the course of which exploration they succeeded in asking not a single question. Upstairs, on the top floor, while the gentleman and lady brought forth those pies and ale (and sundry other pieces of rustic fare) which had been left for them, something moved noiselessly in the darkness and listened.

第三十九章（Stave Three VII）：In Starningham, the tour of the house on Brightwell Road did not take long. As a guide, Mrs Bethlem was thorough but decidedly brisk. It soon became apparent that the house was really in excellent condition, given its circumstances. In many ways, it was a brighter (and surely a cleaner) place now than it had been when Gowing himself had lived here. The rooms were neatly kept and well furnished. If it had something of the air of a kind of museum, preserved but unchanging, then that was surely only a tribute to the industry and honesty of Mrs Bethlem and her late husband. The first floor had three bedrooms, and it was quickly arranged between the captain and the lady as to who should have each chamber. Theirs would be opposite one another, separated by a small landing. If Mrs Bethlem perceived anything indecorous or inappropriate in such an arrangement, then she did not say so. They were even permitted (for, they both later agreed, that is exactly how it felt) to see the uppermost floor. A candle was lit by Mrs Bethlem and given to the captain. He held it out before him while Mrs Fell stood by his side. The space was not so very alarming, even seen by flickering candlelight alone. It was nothing like so clean and tidy as the two storeys which lay below it, but merely a large, unfurnished room. The only attempts at comfort of any kind were three big, frayed rugs which had been laid out upon the wooden floor. A mirror, ornate and framed with silver, hung upon the wall, as it had all those years ago. The place was quite considerably colder than the rest of the house as well. "So this," said the captain, "must be where you slept when you last visited." "It was." "What a very strange choice… To put a child in here." The lady did not reply but only shook her head and turned to leave. With some relief, the pair descended the staircase to the first floor, where Mrs Bethlem was waiting for them with something like vindication glinting in her eyes. After that, there was little enough left for them to do. Besides, the woman seemed eager to be away. Back on the ground floor, in the hallway, Mrs Fell thanked her again for her labours. "You're very welcome," Mrs Bethlem said. "And I know you will be perfectly happy here. You and your little one. So long as you remember what I told you. As to anything else, I dare say you'll just have to discover it on your own. For this is a house of some surprises. Even your uncle found that to be true." "Mrs Bethlem, if I may," began Mrs Fell, only for the other woman to hold up her hand to stop her speech. "I was quite in earnest earlier," she said. "If you wish to ask a question, then please God let us do it outside these walls. I have a few minutes more. And if I can answer them I shall. But, please, please let us do so outside. After that, I must be away. Don't fret. I shall tell you my address in case you should need me again." Another glance was exchanged between the captain and the lady at this, but, of course, Mrs Fell ended by saying that she quite understood. And so the door was opened and the two women retired to the cold of the street outside. For the first time all day, the captain was left alone. He had for many years been by nature a solitary man and he really ought to have enjoyed a few moments of quiet. Yet, on the contrary, he found it difficult not to throw open the door again and go out into the night. For there was – absurd though it might sound – a strong sense that the house in which he stood was curious about him, and that the brand of curiosity was not a friendly or an innocent one. He found that he rather bristled at the notion. Then, speaking in part from bravado and in part from true irritation after a long and often vexing day, he called out: "So why can't we ask any questions in here, then? What's so wrong about asking b—y questions?" It was, perhaps, a mere coincidence, but it sounded very much at that moment as if a single knock was heard, not from outside the house, at door or window, but from somewhere within it. It did not sound like a creak or like a noise which an old house might make when, having been left empty for so long, it suddenly acquires new residents. No, thought the captain, it sounded quite distinctly like the sound of knuckles upon wood. He waited but no further sound could be heard. "Oh, hurry up, Ebenezer," he said defiantly. "In God's name, hurry up and get to Starningham." Then the door opened again and Mrs Fell returned to the house. She was alone. "Mrs Bethlem's gone home," she said by way of explanation. "And I don't know about you, Captain, but I am now very hungry indeed. I think we should eat. And I think also that we should follow Mrs Bethlem's rules." "I think the same," was all that the captain said to this, "and on both counts too." They retired together to the kitchen then, to see what Mrs Bethlem had left them for their supper, in the course of which exploration they succeeded in asking not a single question.
