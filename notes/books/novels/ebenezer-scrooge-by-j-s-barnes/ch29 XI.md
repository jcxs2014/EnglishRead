# 29. XI

## 本章导航

**一句话概括**：There was a voice in the head of poor Mr Beck, one that he could not dislodge. It nagged at him, it pestered, it pleaded and it cajoled. Never before in all of his trafficking with the unseen world had he encountered so persistent and forceful a spirit. He was still uncertain as to what exactly had taken place in the drawing room of Leopold Talbot, but the voice had begun to speak in his head mere moments after he had clambered, groggily, into the cab and called out for it to take him home. It only grew louder after that. Seeking sanctuary in a ring of salt had dulled its incessant questions and demands for a few hours in the night, and the consumption of alcohol had achieved a similar effect once the ring was broken and the dawn had come. Yet these things had worn off soon enough. The voice drowned them all out. Over the hours that had passed since the séance, that voice had become more and more vehement and commanding. And now, ever since Mr Beck had left behind The Jolly Boaters and taken to the streets, its words had become louder and louder in his head until he could barely think, until there was hardly any room left for his own thoughts at all. Desperate and afraid, he took to walking, out of Chancery Lane and into the midst of the city. Even here – even caught up in the bustle and roar of the place – there was no escaping the voice and its orders. His head rang with the sounds of the intruder. He was not drunk, yet he appeared so to passers-by, so uncertain was his gait as he swayed from side to side upon the thoroughfare. More than once he went too far and almost toppled into the gutter. On a single occasion, he came close to repeating the fate of Mrs Fell’s late and largely unlamented husband, and only just succeeded in throwing himself clear of the thundering wheels of a cab. Yet on he went and on, through the streets of the city, until at last he came to St James’s Square and to the door of The Club of Decent-Hearted Men. Here, at last, he stopped. He stood before the door. He did not ring the bell but only waited. For an instant, the voice in his head stopped talking. In the tiny chink of clarity that followed, he wondered where he was and what he was doing there, and why his head ached so abominably. And he wondered also at another thing. The weight that he now felt in the left-hand pocket of his jacket. There was something there which he did not recall having placed there himself, though the preceding hours were not so misty and uncertain. As he stood before the door, he reached down and drew out what he found in his pocket. He held it out before him, wondering why he should have possession of a pistol. Then the door opened. An old man whose face he recognised stood before him. His hand gripped the pistol more tightly. And the voice in his head started up once again. No longer merely talking now or even shouting. But screaming.

## 一句话总结

第二十九章（Stave Three XI）：There was a voice in the head of poor Mr Beck, one that he could not dislodge. It nagged at him, it pestered, it pleaded and it cajoled. Never before in all of his trafficking with the unseen world had he encountered so persistent and forceful a spirit. He was still uncertain as to what exactly had taken place in the drawing room of Leopold Talbot, but the voice had begun to speak in his head mere moments after he had clambered, groggily, into the cab and called out for it to take him home. It only grew louder after that. Seeking sanctuary in a ring of salt had dulled its incessant questions and demands for a few hours in the night, and the consumption of alcohol had achieved a similar effect once the ring was broken and the dawn had come. Yet these things had worn off soon enough. The voice drowned them all out. Over the hours that had passed since the séance, that voice had become more and more vehement and commanding. And now, ever since Mr Beck had left behind The Jolly Boaters and taken to the streets, its words had become louder and louder in his head until he could barely think, until there was hardly any room left for his own thoughts at all. Desperate and afraid, he took to walking, out of Chancery Lane and into the midst of the city. Even here – even caught up in the bustle and roar of the place – there was no escaping the voice and its orders. His head rang with the sounds of the intruder. He was not drunk, yet he appeared so to passers-by, so uncertain was his gait as he swayed from side to side upon the thoroughfare. More than once he went too far and almost toppled into the gutter. On a single occasion, he came close to repeating the fate of Mrs Fell’s late and largely unlamented husband, and only just succeeded in throwing himself clear of the thundering wheels of a cab. Yet on he went and on, through the streets of the city, until at last he came to St James’s Square and to the door of The Club of Decent-Hearted Men. Here, at last, he stopped. He stood before the door. He did not ring the bell but only waited. For an instant, the voice in his head stopped talking. In the tiny chink of clarity that followed, he wondered where he was and what he was doing there, and why his head ached so abominably. And he wondered also at another thing. The weight that he now felt in the left-hand pocket of his jacket. There was something there which he did not recall having placed there himself, though the preceding hours were not so misty and uncertain. As he stood before the door, he reached down and drew out what he found in his pocket. He held it out before him, wondering why he should have possession of a pistol. Then the door opened. An old man whose face he recognised stood before him. His hand gripped the pistol more tightly. And the voice in his head started up once again. No longer merely talking now or even shouting.
