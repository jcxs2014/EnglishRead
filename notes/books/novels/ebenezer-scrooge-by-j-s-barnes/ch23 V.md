# 23. V

## 本章导航

**一句话概括**：The valet, Mr Crawford, cut a flustered and unhappy figure when he burst into the little room in the lower reaches of the hotel that was reserved for the personal use of senior staff; Mr Kett – the oldest employee of the establishment, whose father had once been the personal manservant of the gentleman who had founded the hotel in the last century – was sitting alone in the room, nursing a small glass of something which was most probably sherry; this was, of course, a practice that was by no means accorded to any of those numerous others who were employed by the management – but rank had its privileges for old Mr Kett, together (it was rumoured) with the security that was provided to his position through his knowledge of certain family secrets of long standing; "It's rather early for you, isn't it, Mr Crawford?" This was said with some disapproval but (it must be said) with a modicum of sympathy, for Crawford looked almost unwell as he raced into the room and reached with desperation for the decanter; "Oh it is, Mr Kett, most certainly." He poured himself a glass of the same amber liquid as the old man was drinking. "You may rest assured it's not a thing I intend to make a habit."; "Very glad to hear that," said old Kett. "Very glad indeed." At this, of course, he took a leisured sip from his own full glass; Crawford drank deep, sighed afterwards, and poured himself a second; "Does something ail you, Mr Crawford? I don't believe I've ever seen you drink so fast before. And most certainly not before noon."; at this, of course, Mr Crawford had little choice but to give the older man a brisk sketch of the odd behaviour of the medium, Mr Beck, and of what he had discovered when he set foot into his room; "Salt?" said Mr Kett, though he said the word thoughtfully. His childhood had been spent on a farm in Northamptonshire and he knew well stories about the efficacy of that substance in such a situation as this; "Yes, Mr Kett. Salt. And… in that room… there was a dreadful sense also. Of eyes watching me. And not friendly eyes either. Though I knew very well that there was nothing there."; "Well, Mr Crawford. Nothing visible, that is." And the old man waved his glass in the air for emphasis; "Quite so, Mr Kett. Quite so."; they spoke some more then, about Mr Beck and his reputation, and Mr Kett had a few stories to share from his youth and from his career, which served to allay Mr Crawford's concerns not a jot. It was decided to wait, to follow Mr Beck's instructions and to leave the room undisturbed until the customer's return. After this, he could be dealt with in an appropriate manner, as had other difficult guests before him; at the end of it all, when Mr Crawford remembered both the time and the quantity of his other duties elsewhere in the hotel, he bade farewell to the old man; Mr Kett nodded his goodbyes but, just as Crawford was about to cross the threshold once again, he murmured: "I shall pray for him tonight."; Crawford hesitated in the doorway. "You'll pray for Archie Beck?" he asked; "I shall," said old Mr Kett, who was thinking of certain superstitions and of an odd whispering voice that he had heard once, whilst alone, in the woods as a boy. "And it is my hearty recommendation, sir, that you do the same."

## 一句话总结

第二十三章（Stave Two V）：The valet, Mr Crawford, cut a flustered and unhappy figure when he burst into the little room in the lower reaches of the hotel that was reserved for the personal use of senior staff; Mr Kett – the oldest employee of the establishment, whose father had once been the personal manservant of the gentleman who had founded the hotel in the last century – was sitting alone in the room, nursing a small glass of something which was most probably sherry; this was, of course, a practice that was by no means accorded to any of those numerous others who were employed by the management – but rank had its privileges for old Mr Kett, together (it was rumoured) with the security that was provided to his position through his knowledge of certain family secrets of long standing; "It's rather early for you, isn't it, Mr Crawford?" This was said with some disapproval but (it must be said) with a modicum of sympathy, for Crawford looked almost unwell as he raced into the room and reached with desperation for the decanter; "Oh it is, Mr Kett, most certainly." He poured himself a glass of the same amber liquid as the old man was drinking. "You may rest assured it's not a thing I intend to make a habit."; "Very glad to hear that," said old Kett. "Very glad indeed." At this, of course, he took a leisured sip from his own full glass; Crawford drank deep, sighed afterwards, and poured himself a second; "Does something ail you, Mr Crawford? I don't believe I've ever seen you drink so fast before. And most certainly not before noon."; at this, of course, Mr Crawford had little choice but to give the older man a brisk sketch of the odd behaviour of the medium, Mr Beck, and of what he had discovered when he set foot into his room; "Salt?" said Mr Kett, though he said the word thoughtfully. His childhood had been spent on a farm in Northamptonshire and he knew well stories about the efficacy of that substance in such a situation as this; "Yes, Mr Kett. Salt. And… in that room… there was a dreadful sense also. Of eyes watching me. And not friendly eyes either. Though I knew very well that there was nothing there."; "Well, Mr Crawford. Nothing visible, that is." And the old man waved his glass in the air for emphasis; "Quite so, Mr Kett. Quite so."; they spoke some more then, about Mr Beck and his reputation, and Mr Kett had a few stories to share from his youth and from his career, which served to allay Mr Crawford's concerns not a jot. It was decided to wait, to follow Mr Beck's instructions and to leave the room undisturbed until the customer's return. After this, he could be dealt with in an appropriate manner, as had other difficult guests before him; at the end of it all, when Mr Crawford remembered both the time and the quantity of his other duties elsewhere in the hotel, he bade farewell to the old man; Mr Kett nodded his goodbyes but, just as Crawford was about to cross the threshold once again, he murmured: "I shall pray for him tonight."; Crawford hesitated in the doorway. "You'll pray for Archie Beck?" he asked; "I shall," said old Mr Kett, who was thinking of certain superstitions and of an odd whispering voice that he had heard once, whilst alone, in the woods as a boy.
