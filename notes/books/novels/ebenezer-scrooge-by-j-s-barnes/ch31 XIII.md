# 31. XIII

## 本章导航

**一句话概括**：On the train, Captain Ambrose stopped short, his story not yet finished. He turned pale. He looked into the middle distance. He did not speak. “Captain Ambrose?” prompted the lady. “Are you quite all right?” “I…” This single syllable spoken, the soldier lapsed into silence once again. Then, collecting himself with what seemed to the other passenger like quite an effort of will: “Yes, quite all right, thank you. I just had the strangest sensation. You know the old saying, of course, about a goose walking over your grave?” The lady said that she did. “I don’t believe I’ve ever felt it before quite so intensely.” He shrugged. “I’m sure that it most likely signifies nothing at all.” “Perhaps.” Mrs Fell, who did not sound convinced by this argument, looked at him levelly. “We shall surely see. But for now – please – finish your story. About poor Mr Halvorsen.” Ambrose, who had until then just been beginning to enjoy himself, found that his enthusiasm was waning. He did not wish to say more to the lady or to alarm her in any way, but he felt an odd certainty that all was not well. Nonetheless, seeing no alternative but to push down these unexpected sentiments, he went on with his tale, being sure to place particular emphasis on the successful conclusion to the case so as to reassure his companion. “Where was I? Oh yes – Mr Scrooge and I, dogging the steps of the unfortunate Halvorsen, had just seen that spectre which had attached itself to him, glaring at us out of the gloom, its little face a mask of hatred and resentment. At the sight of it, old Scrooge – who is, I think, the most courageous man that I have ever met – sprang forward and began to run towards the creature, heedless of his safety. I was only a few seconds behind him, but as we approached, the entity vanished as swiftly and as completely as any stage illusion. Mr Halvorsen himself was startled at our approach but also, I think, rather relieved. At this point, we three gentlemen retired to a nearby tavern, in which establishment we were able to assure our new friend that we had seen the creature, that we believed his story utterly and that our every effort was now to be expended upon releasing him from this horrible persecution. He thanked us, but he then seemed somewhat surprised when Mr Scrooge instructed him to turn out his pockets.” “Why ever did he do that?” asked the lady. “He wanted to see if there was anything about Mr Halvorsen’s person which was attracting that malicious spirit.” “And was there?” “There was. For it transpired that our new friend was something of a mudlark. One of those who take pleasure in combing the banks of the Thames for flotsam and treasure.” “Ah. And what had he picked up?” “Many things, ma’am. He was a little embarrassed – as well he might be – by the wealth of items that he was in the habit of carrying about his person. Yet there was one thing in particular… Mr Scrooge has a sensitivity in such matters, you understand, and he saw it at once – a tarnished silver coin from many centuries past. A Roman thing. A denarius, I believe it’s called. Had a picture on it of one of their strange gods. And it seemed – in Mr Scrooge’s opinion – that it was this object which was attracting the malevolent thing that was following old Halvorsen all around the town. For some spirits, ma’am, they attach themselves to objects. Others attach themselves to people. And others still are connected to…” “Houses,” said Mrs Fell quietly. “Yes, ma’am.” “And so what happened after that?” “In the end, it was Scrooge who finished the matter. We drained our glasses and then went down, the three of us, to that patch of the Thames where Halvorsen believed he had found it. I thought I knew what Ebenezer’s plan would be. He reached out an arm as if to throw the coin as far as he could into the murky waters of the Thames. I moved towards him and was about to suggest, as tactfully as I could, that the job should, perhaps, be mine, since I am surely a more powerful bowler than he. Divining my intention, he waved me away. “‘We’re not alone here,’ he said. ‘There is another.’ “‘Mr Halvorsen?’ “He shook his head, his meaning plain. Not him. Another still. “Then Scrooge threw the coin high up into the air – straight up, not towards the water. “I am still far from certain, Mrs Fell, what happened then. Except to say that I saw a glimpse of something, an impossible thing, a flutter of something like dark fabric against the clear sky. Of the coin there was no sign at all. “‘What’s happened?’ Halvorsen asked. ‘What was that?’ “Scrooge only smiled. He held out his palm. Three long seconds later and the coin fell back into it, from thin air so far as I was able to tell. Where it had been I could not say. ‘All done,’ said the old man. ‘All finished.’ “Then he tucked the coin away in his pocket. And shook hands with Mr Halvorsen.” “And so was that the end of it?” “So far as I can tell, ma’am, yes. Halvorsen came to see us once again, a month and a half later, just before he was due to board a ship that was bound for Johannesburg. He thanked us both and shook us by the hand and said there had been no recurrence of the phenomenon. He seemed better too. Lighter and happier in himself.” “I am pleased to hear that,” said Mrs Fell. “Though I doubt very much my own story will be so neatly resolved. Don’t you, Captain?” Ambrose, being uncertain as to how to respond to this statement, only nodded sympathetically. Quiet fell between them once again after that, and the train roared on, moving with impatient speed towards the very edge of England.

## 一句话总结

第三十一章（Stave Three XIII）：On the train, Captain Ambrose stopped short, his story not yet finished. He turned pale. He looked into the middle distance. He did not speak. “Captain Ambrose?” prompted the lady. “Are you quite all right?” “I…” This single syllable spoken, the soldier lapsed into silence once again. Then, collecting himself with what seemed to the other passenger like quite an effort of will: “Yes, quite all right, thank you. I just had the strangest sensation. You know the old saying, of course, about a goose walking over your grave?” The lady said that she did. “I don’t believe I’ve ever felt it before quite so intensely.” He shrugged. “I’m sure that it most likely signifies nothing at all.” “Perhaps.” Mrs Fell, who did not sound convinced by this argument, looked at him levelly. “We shall surely see. But for now – please – finish your story. About poor Mr Halvorsen.” Ambrose, who had until then just been beginning to enjoy himself, found that his enthusiasm was waning. He did not wish to say more to the lady or to alarm her in any way, but he felt an odd certainty that all was not well. Nonetheless, seeing no alternative but to push down these unexpected sentiments, he went on with his tale, being sure to place particular emphasis on the successful conclusion to the case so as to reassure his companion. “Where was I? Oh yes – Mr Scrooge and I, dogging the steps of the unfortunate Halvorsen, had just seen that spectre which had attached itself to him, glaring at us out of the gloom, its little face a mask of hatred and resentment. At the sight of it, old Scrooge – who is, I think, the most courageous man that I have ever met – sprang forward and began to run towards the creature, heedless of his safety. I was only a few seconds behind him, but as we approached, the entity vanished as swiftly and as completely as any stage illusion. Mr Halvorsen himself was startled at our approach but also, I think, rather relieved. At this point, we three gentlemen retired to a nearby tavern, in which establishment we were able to assure our new friend that we had seen the creature, that we believed his story utterly and that our every effort was now to be expended upon releasing him from this horrible persecution. He thanked us, but he then seemed somewhat surprised when Mr Scrooge instructed him to turn out his pockets.” “Why ever did he do that?” asked the lady. “He wanted to see if there was anything about Mr Halvorsen’s person which was attracting that malicious spirit.” “And was there?” “There was. For it transpired that our new friend was something of a mudlark. One of those who take pleasure in combing the banks of the Thames for flotsam and treasure.” “Ah. And what had he picked up?” “Many things, ma’am. He was a little embarrassed – as well he might be – by the wealth of items that he was in the habit of carrying about his person. Yet there was one thing in particular… Mr Scrooge has a sensitivity in such matters, you understand, and he saw it at once – a tarnished silver coin from many centuries past. A Roman thing. A denarius, I believe it’s called. Had a picture on it of one of their strange gods. And it seemed – in Mr Scrooge’s opinion – that it was this object which was attracting the malevolent thing that was following old Halvorsen all around the town. For some spirits, ma’am, they attach themselves to objects. Others attach themselves to people. And others still are connected to…” “Houses,” said Mrs Fell quietly. “Yes, ma’am.” “And so what happened after that?” “In the end, it was Scrooge who finished the matter. We drained our glasses and then went down, the three of us, to that patch of the Thames where Halvorsen believed he had found it. I thought I knew what Ebenezer’s plan would be. He reached out an arm as if to throw the coin as far as he could into the murky waters of the Thames. I moved towards him and was about to suggest, as tactfully as I could, that the job should, perhaps, be mine, since I am surely a more powerful bowler than he. Divining my intention, he waved me away. “‘We’re not alone here,’ he said. ‘There is another.’ “‘Mr Halvorsen?’ “He shook his head, his meaning plain. Not him. Another still. “Then Scrooge threw the coin high up into the air – straight up, not towards the water. “I am still far from certain, Mrs Fell, what happened then. Except to say that I saw a glimpse of something, an impossible thing, a flutter of something like dark fabric against the clear sky. Of the coin there was no sign at all. “‘What’s happened?’ Halvorsen asked. ‘What was that?’ “Scrooge only smiled. He held out his palm. Three long seconds later and the coin fell back into it, from thin air so far as I was able to tell. Where it had been I could not say. ‘All done,’ said the old man. ‘All finished.’ “Then he tucked the coin away in his pocket. And shook hands with Mr Halvorsen.” “And so was that the end of it?” “So far as I can tell, ma’am, yes. Halvorsen came to see us once again, a month and a half later, just before he was due to board a ship that was bound for Johannesburg. He thanked us both and shook us by the hand and said there had been no recurrence of the phenomenon. He seemed better too. Lighter and happier in himself.” “I am pleased to hear that,” said Mrs Fell. “Though I doubt very much my own story will be so neatly resolved.
