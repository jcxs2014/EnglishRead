# 38. VI

## 本章导航

**一句话概括**：Outside that makeshift sick room in The Club of Decent-Hearted Men, three people stood and waited anxiously: Mr Bentinck, the Club Secretary; Charlotte, the maid (given leave to abandon all of her duties for the time being); and Mr Penberry, the passer-by who had now become caught up in the tragedy of the evening. The second passer-by – the newspaper seller – had by then made his apologies and left, having done all that he could and having a great deal of other responsibilities which pressed for his attention. He had pledged, however, to carry out one other good deed: to inform the authorities of this dreadful incident and to implore them to search for Scrooge's assailant, although no satisfactory description of that gentleman had yet been obtained. "I only saw the back of him," poor Charlotte had said when asked if she could recollect anything in the line of useful detail. "A man, of course. A young one, I would say. With dark hair. He moved fast but seemed… panicked, somehow." All of this the man had committed to memory and departed, over an hour ago. A representative of the law had yet to arrive. The doctor had been sequestered with Mr Scrooge for this whole while. "We should knock," said Penberry, who had spent much of the intervening time since his return with the doctor, pacing up and down and consulting his pocket watch. "We should see if he needs our help." Bentinck waved this suggestion away. "Tarkin's a good man. He'll say if he needs us." Charlotte sat very quietly, waiting for news, while the men talked amongst themselves. At long last, just around the time that Mrs Fell and Captain Ambrose were being shown around the house on Brightwell Road by Mrs Bethlem, the door to the office was opened and the doctor strode out. His hands were bloody and his face was grave. He had a small, sturdy object tucked under his right arm. "Well?" Bentinck asked. The doctor took a deep breath, steadying himself before the delivery of news. "Ebenezer Scrooge," he said, "still lives." At this – many smiles and expressions of optimism. "He has been fortunate," the physician went on. "The path of the bullet was slowed significantly… by this." And with something of a conjuror's flourish, he showed them the object that he had beneath his arm: a small leather-bound Bible. The front of it had been wrenched away, and many of the pages within were reduced to so many shreds. "He has been wounded and the bleeding was substantial. I have removed the bullet. Washed and darned the wound. He has yet to wake and sleeps still. But without this…" He held up once again the book. "Without this he would surely be dead." Charlotte murmured that this was surely a miracle and declared it to be an answer to their prayers. "Would you like to see him?" asked the doctor, a thing to which the others heartily assented. Inside, the old man was lying very still upon the bloodied bed. His eyes were closed. His breathing was ragged. Charlotte went in first to see him, and at the sight of how shrunken and reduced he seemed, and how fragile his grip upon life seemed to be, she could not help but gasp in pity and in horror. She went to his side. She held his right hand. "Oh, Mr Scrooge, sir, Mr Scrooge!" The others came in after her and bore witness to the scene, sorrowful and with heads bowed. That is how we shall leave them for the time being: the three of them in mournful tableau around the body of the stricken man. And with Mr Scrooge himself, still sleeping, lost and in darkness.

## 一句话总结

第三十八章（Stave Three VI）：Outside that makeshift sick room in The Club of Decent-Hearted Men, three people stood and waited anxiously: Mr Bentinck, the Club Secretary; Charlotte, the maid (given leave to abandon all of her duties for the time being); and Mr Penberry, the passer-by who had now become caught up in the tragedy of the evening. The second passer-by – the newspaper seller – had by then made his apologies and left, having done all that he could and having a great deal of other responsibilities which pressed for his attention. He had pledged, however, to carry out one other good deed: to inform the authorities of this dreadful incident and to implore them to search for Scrooge's assailant, although no satisfactory description of that gentleman had yet been obtained. "I only saw the back of him," poor Charlotte had said when asked if she could recollect anything in the line of useful detail. "A man, of course. A young one, I would say. With dark hair. He moved fast but seemed… panicked, somehow." All of this the man had committed to memory and departed, over an hour ago. A representative of the law had yet to arrive. The doctor had been sequestered with Mr Scrooge for this whole while. "We should knock," said Penberry, who had spent much of the intervening time since his return with the doctor, pacing up and down and consulting his pocket watch. "We should see if he needs our help." Bentinck waved this suggestion away. "Tarkin's a good man. He'll say if he needs us." Charlotte sat very quietly, waiting for news, while the men talked amongst themselves. At long last, just around the time that Mrs Fell and Captain Ambrose were being shown around the house on Brightwell Road by Mrs Bethlem, the door to the office was opened and the doctor strode out. His hands were bloody and his face was grave. He had a small, sturdy object tucked under his right arm. "Well?" Bentinck asked. The doctor took a deep breath, steadying himself before the delivery of news. "Ebenezer Scrooge," he said, "still lives." At this – many smiles and expressions of optimism. "He has been fortunate," the physician went on. "The path of the bullet was slowed significantly… by this." And with something of a conjuror's flourish, he showed them the object that he had beneath his arm: a small leather-bound Bible. The front of it had been wrenched away, and many of the pages within were reduced to so many shreds. "He has been wounded and the bleeding was substantial. I have removed the bullet. Washed and darned the wound. He has yet to wake and sleeps still. But without this…" He held up once again the book. "Without this he would surely be dead." Charlotte murmured that this was surely a miracle and declared it to be an answer to their prayers. "Would you like to see him?" asked the doctor, a thing to which the others heartily assented. Inside, the old man was lying very still upon the bloodied bed. His eyes were closed. His breathing was ragged. Charlotte went in first to see him, and at the sight of how shrunken and reduced he seemed, and how fragile his grip upon life seemed to be, she could not help but gasp in pity and in horror. She went to his side. She held his right hand. "Oh, Mr Scrooge, sir, Mr Scrooge!" The others came in after her and bore witness to the scene, sorrowful and with heads bowed. That is how we shall leave them for the time being: the three of them in mournful tableau around the body of the stricken man.
