# 25. VII

## 本章导航

**一句话概括**：On the train, Captain Ambrose leaned forward and looked Mrs Fell directly in the eyes; the telling of the story seemed to have given him a degree of additional confidence in this regard; "Now, of course," he went on, "I brought the client inside and carried him through to Mr Scrooge's room. We took off his greatcoat and gave him some water to drink and a minute or so later the fellow came around. And very embarrassed he looked indeed, to have made his entrance into our lives in such a fashion. We bade him take his time to recover and not to try to speak if he were still feeling faint, but he blushed. Of course, the old man was very charming at this and offered the fellow the use of the chamber in which you yourself slept last night, Mrs Fell. But the fellow sat upright in the chair and began to tell us his story."; "His name, he said, was Halvorsen. His English was very good, tinged with a slight accent. He had been born in Sweden and had come to England as a sailor. His work had often brought him to these shores and, over the years, he had come to think of it as more of a home than the nation of his birth. He had not been on a ship, he said, for almost three years now. There was much that was rather mysterious about his situation. He seemed close to being itinerant, moving from one lodging house to the next. Of any job or employment there was no mention. Perhaps he lived, in some meagre fashion, off his savings? Yet for all of that, he struck us both as a sane man. A rational man. A man whose word could be trusted. Yet he was haunted, he said – haunted by a figure he did not know or recognise but which dogged his every footstep."; "For, madam, this was a most unusual thing – unusual even in the experience of Mr Scrooge – a portable haunting! He was followed, he said, in the hours of darkness wherever he was and wherever he went. There was someone beside him from the moment that twilight began to descend until the first glimmerings of the dawn."; "Of course, we asked him to describe this personage who seemed to have taken such an interest in him, at which poor Halvorsen grew more and more distressed. Still, after the application of a little brandy, he was able to give us some rough description: a small man of about his own age, very lean and sinewy and dressed in rags. This did not sound so very terrifying, save for our client's description of this fellow's eyes: deep-set, red-rimmed and glinting with malice. They seemed always to emerge from even the thickest darkness, he said, like two orbs, burning in the gloom."; now the lady interrupted. "Was he quite certain that this man who pursued him wasn't simply a real, living person? Surely London is full of the most peculiar and desperate people? Was it not possible that someone had simply taken an interest in Mr Halvorsen and chosen to hound him?"; "Of course, Mr Scrooge and I had the same thought. But Halvorsen was quite determined. The spectre was no living man but something which had died and refused to leave the world. He could follow him anywhere, Halvorsen said, even into locked rooms and empty places. He had been woken more times than he could count in the middle of the night by the sight of this weird stranger watching him from the shadows. He followed him through the streets, taverns, lodging houses and even, on one occasion, into a hotel in which he had bought himself a single night of sanctuary. The only place where he had a respite in the hours of darkness from this persistent thing was within the walls of a church, beyond which the apparition did not seem to wish to venture."; "Mr Scrooge seemed quite fascinated by the case and had many questions for our visitor. Was there any reason that he should be haunted in this fashion? Halvorsen could think of none. Had he ever tried to speak to the spectre, or it to him? No words had ever been exchanged, Halvorsen said, but there seemed to exist between them an awful understanding. Did Mr Halvorsen think that he was connected personally to this being? Or had it simply picked him out on something like a whim? According to Mr Halvorsen the haunting seemed to be a question of pure chance. Though Mr Scrooge was less convinced. 'There is always a reason', he says, 'why a man or a woman might be haunted. And oftentimes a very good reason.' Perhaps one day, ma'am, the old man might even tell you why he says such things and how they are born of his own experience."; "I should like that," interjected the lady. "Indeed I would. But, for now, you must tell me what happened with Mr Halvorsen. What did Mr Scrooge do next to help him?"; "Well, ma'am," said the captain. "We spoke some more and persuaded Halvorsen to take a little luncheon with us. And then we hatched a plan. A plan, and perhaps a kind of trap also. And then we waited, the three of us, for the arrival of the night."

## 一句话总结

第二十五章（Stave Three VII）：On the train, Captain Ambrose leaned forward and looked Mrs Fell directly in the eyes; the telling of the story seemed to have given him a degree of additional confidence in this regard; "Now, of course," he went on, "I brought the client inside and carried him through to Mr Scrooge's room. We took off his greatcoat and gave him some water to drink and a minute or so later the fellow came around. And very embarrassed he looked indeed, to have made his entrance into our lives in such a fashion. We bade him take his time to recover and not to try to speak if he were still feeling faint, but he blushed. Of course, the old man was very charming at this and offered the fellow the use of the chamber in which you yourself slept last night, Mrs Fell. But the fellow sat upright in the chair and began to tell us his story."; "His name, he said, was Halvorsen. His English was very good, tinged with a slight accent. He had been born in Sweden and had come to England as a sailor. His work had often brought him to these shores and, over the years, he had come to think of it as more of a home than the nation of his birth. He had not been on a ship, he said, for almost three years now. There was much that was rather mysterious about his situation. He seemed close to being itinerant, moving from one lodging house to the next. Of any job or employment there was no mention. Perhaps he lived, in some meagre fashion, off his savings? Yet for all of that, he struck us both as a sane man. A rational man. A man whose word could be trusted. Yet he was haunted, he said – haunted by a figure he did not know or recognise but which dogged his every footstep."; "For, madam, this was a most unusual thing – unusual even in the experience of Mr Scrooge – a portable haunting! He was followed, he said, in the hours of darkness wherever he was and wherever he went. There was someone beside him from the moment that twilight began to descend until the first glimmerings of the dawn."; "Of course, we asked him to describe this personage who seemed to have taken such an interest in him, at which poor Halvorsen grew more and more distressed. Still, after the application of a little brandy, he was able to give us some rough description: a small man of about his own age, very lean and sinewy and dressed in rags. This did not sound so very terrifying, save for our client's description of this fellow's eyes: deep-set, red-rimmed and glinting with malice. They seemed always to emerge from even the thickest darkness, he said, like two orbs, burning in the gloom."; now the lady interrupted. "Was he quite certain that this man who pursued him wasn't simply a real, living person? Surely London is full of the most peculiar and desperate people? Was it not possible that someone had simply taken an interest in Mr Halvorsen and chosen to hound him?"; "Of course, Mr Scrooge and I had the same thought. But Halvorsen was quite determined. The spectre was no living man but something which had died and refused to leave the world. He could follow him anywhere, Halvorsen said, even into locked rooms and empty places. He had been woken more times than he could count in the middle of the night by the sight of this weird stranger watching him from the shadows. He followed him through the streets, taverns, lodging houses and even, on one occasion, into a hotel in which he had bought himself a single night of sanctuary. The only place where he had a respite in the hours of darkness from this persistent thing was within the walls of a church, beyond which the apparition did not seem to wish to venture."; "Mr Scrooge seemed quite fascinated by the case and had many questions for our visitor. Was there any reason that he should be haunted in this fashion? Halvorsen could think of none. Had he ever tried to speak to the spectre, or it to him? No words had ever been exchanged, Halvorsen said, but there seemed to exist between them an awful understanding. Did Mr Halvorsen think that he was connected personally to this being? Or had it simply picked him out on something like a whim? According to Mr Halvorsen the haunting seemed to be a question of pure chance. Though Mr Scrooge was less convinced. 'There is always a reason', he says, 'why a man or a woman might be haunted. And oftentimes a very good reason.' Perhaps one day, ma'am, the old man might even tell you why he says such things and how they are born of his own experience."; "I should like that," interjected the lady. "Indeed I would. But, for now, you must tell me what happened with Mr Halvorsen. What did Mr Scrooge do next to help him?"; "Well, ma'am," said the captain.
