# 24. VI

## 本章导航

"And so, once again: thank you."; the other man nodded; he stood beside the same gravestone as that which Ebenezer had come to visit; "Forgive me, sir," said the man, "but don't I know you? It is Mr Scrooge, isn't it?"; "It is, sir. Yes."; "Jack Trelawney." The man extended his hand and Scrooge shook it; "Ah," said Scrooge, thinking of the last time that he had seen the gentleman in question, in a kind of vision that had been presented to him by a spirit on the night before Christmas some years before. "You were her husband," he said, as though he did not already know the answer; "I was, sir, yes, and most fortunate I was indeed to have that honour."; "You were, Mr Trelawney. You were."; they looked at one another for a long minute after that; age, I fear to relate, does not make encounters such as these any easier to bear; "She mentioned you from time to time," he said. "Always with great kindness when she did so."; Mr Scrooge – who suspected this of being a benign piece of fiction – murmured that such remarks demonstrated the excellence of the lady's character; Mr Trelawney agreed that this was so; "I hope you'll forgive me for saying so, sir," said the widower, "but you look… different somehow than when I glimpsed you last."; "Oh, I have been changed," Scrooge said, "yes. Transformed entirely."; "Well, that's very good, I expect."; "Yes, Mr Trelawney. Yes, it is."; "And how do you spend your days now, sir? No doubt you're knee-deep in matters of business, just as much as ever you were?"; Mr Scrooge smiled then, for the first time that morning. "Oh, I find that business rather takes care of itself nowadays."; "Then you are doubtless enjoying your retirement?"; "I'm not yet retired, sir. Nor do I have any expectation of becoming so. For I have a mighty debt to work off. Much to sacrifice and many yet to help and protect. Speaking of which, I must away. I have made a promise to travel to Norfolk by nightfall."; Trelawney wished Scrooge luck with all of this and said how nice it was to have met him properly at last (a thing which was not entirely true but nonetheless the kind of remark which convention dictates is made by the graveside); "Can I say something, sir?" said Scrooge, just before they parted; "Of course."; "You're very fortunate, sir. You really were. And are. I only wish that I had seen the truth much earlier in life, as, evidently, did you."; Trelawney smiled a deep, kind smile. "We all of us realise what we are meant to realise only when we are supposed to realise it. Dear me, what a tangled sentence. But you comprehend my meaning?"; "I do, sir. Yes. Indeed, I do. Providence works at a pace that is all its own."; Trelawney agreed that this was so before something seemed to occur to him. As if acting on a whim, he reached into his pocket and took out a small, leather-bound book. "Here," he said. "I would like you to have this. Or, rather, I have the sudden sensation that she would have wanted you to have this. Belle, I mean."; almost gingerly, Scrooge reached out to take it. "What is it?" he asked; "It was her Bible. She always had it with her."; Scrooge touched the cover of the book. "I can't…" he said. "I can't possibly accept this. It must be very precious to you."; "I think," began Trelawney, "that it really ought to be yours now."; "Why?" asked Ebenezer; "Do you know, I'm not entirely certain? Seeing you here today after all these years… The dog… And, well, providence, I suppose."; and after that, all Scrooge could do was to say thank you and to accept this gift and stow it away in the right-hand pocket of his jacket, and to shake Trelawney's hand. "Thank you," he said once again upon his departure; Mr Trelawney lingered there a while longer and murmured a prayer over the grave. This done, he looked up again and watched the figure of the old man disappear back into the city. As he did so, Trelawney witnessed a moment of oddity. For an instant, he thought that he saw the black dog again, padding behind Ebenezer as if in pursuit of him. Then he blinked, rubbed his eyes and looked again only to see that both man and hound (if it had even been there at all) had vanished.

## 一句话总结

第二十四章（Stave Two VI）：In the cemetery, Ebenezer Scrooge turned to face the animal that had so rudely interrupted his reverie; the beast was lean and black, teeth bared, as the old man turned to face it, the creature snarled once again; you may take it from me that even the most ardent admirer of canine kind – the greatest enthusiast alive for the companionship that may be provided to Man by the best of dogs – would have flinched at the sight of this dark brute; another snarl; Scrooge stayed stock still, knowing better than to back away; at that time, wild dogs were still by no means unknown in the viler quarters of the city, and it was of these dangerous aberrations that the old man first took it to be; then the animal snapped its jaws and made as if to spring; the old man was on the edge of flight and trusting to providence that he could outpace the creature (a feat which seemed most unlikely) when he heard a voice call out: "Hi! Hi! Away with you!"; behind the dog appeared another man, some few years younger than Scrooge, though looking in rather ruder health; he held in his right hand a stout stick which he now held upright as if he meant to strike the dog in self-defence; at this approach, and with a final snarl (which sounded to Scrooge altogether too much like frustration), the dog loped away; the stranger approached, and as he did so, Scrooge realised that he was not entirely a stranger to him for all that they had never previously spoken; the man raised his hand in greeting; "I owe you my thanks," said Scrooge, perhaps a little more frostily than he might have wished; "Oh, I doubt he would have done you any serious mischief," said the newcomer. "Just hungry, I expect."; "I am not so sure about that," said Scrooge. "And so, once again: thank you."; the other man nodded; he stood beside the same gravestone as that which Ebenezer had come to visit; "Forgive me, sir," said the man, "but don't I know you? It is Mr Scrooge, isn't it?"; "It is, sir. Yes."; "Jack Trelawney." The man extended his hand and Scrooge shook it; "Ah," said Scrooge, thinking of the last time that he had seen the gentleman in question, in a kind of vision that had been presented to him by a spirit on the night before Christmas some years before. "You were her husband," he said, as though he did not already know the answer; "I was, sir, yes, and most fortunate I was indeed to have that honour."; "You were, Mr Trelawney. You were."; they looked at one another for a long minute after that; age, I fear to relate, does not make encounters such as these any easier to bear; "She mentioned you from time to time," he said. "Oh, I find that business rather takes care of itself nowadays."; "Then you are doubtless enjoying your retirement?"; "I'm not yet retired, sir. Nor do I have any expectation of becoming so. I have made a promise to travel to Norfolk by nightfall."; Trelawney wished Scrooge luck with all of this and said how nice it was to have met him properly at last (a thing which was not entirely true but nonetheless the kind of remark which convention dictates is made by the graveside); "Can I say something, sir?" said Scrooge, just before they parted; "Of course."; "You're very fortunate, sir. You really were. And are. I only wish that I had seen the truth much earlier in life, as, evidently, did you."; Trelawney smiled a deep, kind smile. "We all of us realise what we are meant to realise only when we are supposed to realise it. Dear me, what a tangled sentence. But you comprehend my meaning?"; "I do, sir. Yes. Indeed, I do. Providence works at a pace that is all its own."; Trelawney agreed that this was so before something seemed to occur to him. She always had it with her."; Scrooge touched the cover of the book. "I can't…" he said. "I can't possibly accept this.
