# 36. IV

## 本章导航

**一句话概括**：They walked together down Brightwell Road, the captain and the lady, hand in hand once again, now behind Mrs Bethlem. "Is it as you remember?" Ambrose asked. "Yes, I think so," said Mrs Fell. "A quiet, still sort of street. It seems very peaceful after London, don't you think?" The captain agreed that this was so. He thought (though he did not speak this thought aloud) that a peaceful surface does not always indicate those dangerous undercurrents which might well lie beneath. By then, the group had passed four or five Starningham residents, but they were all unremarkable enough. The men tapped the brims of their hats in greeting to the ladies, while the women smiled and said good evening and weren't we lucky it was still so dry given the time of year. Folk were friendlier here than they were in London, but that was by no means unusual for the provinces. Certainly, there seemed to be no especially sinister air to any of the passers-by or to any of the other houses which lay in a rough, curved line along Brightwell Road. Somewhat to his relief (and only very slightly to his disappointment), the captain – who had seen a good many spirits in his time, or at least whatever it is that men call spirits – felt nothing at all in terms of any promise of the supernatural. No chill (except for the cold of the evening). No sense of unease. No sense of being watched by unseen eyes. He was even beginning to wonder if the whole thing wasn't simply some elaborate piece of shared fantasy, cooked up by the late Mr Gowing, then aided and abetted by Mr Cadge, Mr Beck and by that mysterious (and, he had always thought, highly unreliable) personage known as 'Giles'. And then he saw the house. The last one upon the street. It emanated malice. If a house might be said to possess the architectural equivalent of a snarl, then this assuredly was it. Three storeys, dark windows, standing away from the other properties, with the spire of the church rising behind it. The place was as it had been described to the captain, though no description had come close to capturing its quality of focused malevolence. And all at once the captain felt very glad indeed that he had accompanied Mrs Fell to this place, for he would not for all the world have abandoned the lady (and the precious life which she bore within her) to step over the threshold of that building alone. "So, this is it then," he said to the lady at his side. "It certainly hasn't changed," murmured Louisa. "I thought it would be more… tumbledown somehow. It's been almost a decade, after all, since anyone has lived in it." "Well, I have cared for it," interjected Mrs Bethlem. "Mr Gowing made a most generous allowance in his will for that. For the past ten years I have come into this house three times a week. I have cleaned it. I have kept it secure. I have lit fires in the winter and aired the place at the height of summer. When my dear husband was still alive, he would aid me in these endeavours. For the past three years, since his sad passing, I have done so alone." "I see." Mrs Fell looked up at the house. She shivered. "As of this evening," Mrs Bethlem went on, "that arrangement is at an end. Mr Cadge of Cleeford, Crawlin and Cadge has arranged for me to receive my final payment from the estate of Mr Gowing. This house, my dear Mrs Fell, is now yours. It is your responsibility and yours alone. Would you like to try the key, dear?" Others, at such a moment at this, would have hesitated, but Mrs Fell was made of sterner stuff. Without looking at either of her companions, she walked swiftly to the door. She took out the key, slid it into place and found that it turned smoothly in the lock (another useful legacy, no doubt, from the care which had been provided to the place by Mrs Bethlem and her late husband). It was an odd feeling for her, opening wide the door and watching it swing backwards. If you had asked her to predict at any time since she had received the letter from Cadge informing her of her inheritance, she would never have foreseen the truth: that to walk into the house of Brightwell Road felt peculiarly like coming home. For an instant, she even imagined that her own dear father was somewhere within and that he might come forward out of the gloom to envelop her in his strong arms as in days of old. Though, of course, this was quite impossible and she shook off this piece of fancy immediately. She must have seemed to hesitate upon the threshold of the house, as she heard the captain call out behind her. "Are you quite all right, ma'am?" Why, the fellow seemed to have asked her this some three dozen times that day alone. "Oh, I'm quite well!" she called back, with a casual sort of courage in her voice which she did not altogether feel. Then she stepped forward, into the shadows of the house, and Captain Ambrose and Mrs Bethlem followed on behind.

## 一句话总结

第三十六章（Stave Three IV）：They walked together down Brightwell Road, the captain and the lady, hand in hand once again, now behind Mrs Bethlem. "Is it as you remember?" Ambrose asked. "Yes, I think so," said Mrs Fell. "A quiet, still sort of street. It seems very peaceful after London, don't you think?" The captain agreed that this was so. He thought (though he did not speak this thought aloud) that a peaceful surface does not always indicate those dangerous undercurrents which might well lie beneath. By then, the group had passed four or five Starningham residents, but they were all unremarkable enough. The men tapped the brims of their hats in greeting to the ladies, while the women smiled and said good evening and weren't we lucky it was still so dry given the time of year. Folk were friendlier here than they were in London, but that was by no means unusual for the provinces. Certainly, there seemed to be no especially sinister air to any of the passers-by or to any of the other houses which lay in a rough, curved line along Brightwell Road. Somewhat to his relief (and only very slightly to his disappointment), the captain – who had seen a good many spirits in his time, or at least whatever it is that men call spirits – felt nothing at all in terms of any promise of the supernatural. No chill (except for the cold of the evening). No sense of unease. No sense of being watched by unseen eyes. He was even beginning to wonder if the whole thing wasn't simply some elaborate piece of shared fantasy, cooked up by the late Mr Gowing, then aided and abetted by Mr Cadge, Mr Beck and by that mysterious (and, he had always thought, highly unreliable) personage known as 'Giles'. And then he saw the house. The last one upon the street. It emanated malice. If a house might be said to possess the architectural equivalent of a snarl, then this assuredly was it. Three storeys, dark windows, standing away from the other properties, with the spire of the church rising behind it. The place was as it had been described to the captain, though no description had come close to capturing its quality of focused malevolence. And all at once the captain felt very glad indeed that he had accompanied Mrs Fell to this place, for he would not for all the world have abandoned the lady (and the precious life which she bore within her) to step over the threshold of that building alone. "So, this is it then," he said to the lady at his side. "It certainly hasn't changed," murmured Louisa. "I thought it would be more… tumbledown somehow. It's been almost a decade, after all, since anyone has lived in it." "Well, I have cared for it," interjected Mrs Bethlem. "Mr Gowing made a most generous allowance in his will for that. For the past ten years I have come into this house three times a week. I have cleaned it. I have kept it secure. I have lit fires in the winter and aired the place at the height of summer. When my dear husband was still alive, he would aid me in these endeavours. For the past three years, since his sad passing, I have done so alone." "I see." Mrs Fell looked up at the house. She shivered. "As of this evening," Mrs Bethlem went on, "that arrangement is at an end. Mr Cadge of Cleeford, Crawlin and Cadge has arranged for me to receive my final payment from the estate of Mr Gowing. This house, my dear Mrs Fell, is now yours. It is your responsibility and yours alone. Would you like to try the key, dear?" Others, at such a moment at this, would have hesitated, but Mrs Fell was made of sterner stuff. Without looking at either of her companions, she walked swiftly to the door. She took out the key, slid it into place and found that it turned smoothly in the lock (another useful legacy, no doubt, from the care which had been provided to the place by Mrs Bethlem and her late husband). It was an odd feeling for her, opening wide the door and watching it swing backwards. If you had asked her to predict at any time since she had received the letter from Cadge informing her of her inheritance, she would never have foreseen the truth: that to walk into the house of Brightwell Road felt peculiarly like coming home. For an instant, she even imagined that her own dear father was somewhere within and that he might come forward out of the gloom to envelop her in his strong arms as in days of old. Though, of course, this was quite impossible and she shook off this piece of fancy immediately. She must have seemed to hesitate upon the threshold of the house, as she heard the captain call out behind her. "Are you quite all right, ma'am?" Why, the fellow seemed to have asked her this some three dozen times that day alone. "Oh, I'm quite well!" she called back, with a casual sort of courage in her voice which she did not altogether feel.
