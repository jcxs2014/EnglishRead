# 33. I

## 本章导航

**一句话概括**：What a scene there was played out upon the steps of The Club of Decent-Hearted Men! Mr Beck had fled back into the dreadful security of the city, leaving Ebenezer sprawled and wounded upon the threshold. Charlotte – that indefatigable maidservant – cradled the old man in her arms, calling for help. With commendable alacrity, the Club's secretary, Mr Bentinck, was out in the open air almost at once, to seek the reason for the commotion. Long ago, he had himself served his country in a military capacity (without, it must be said, a great deal of distinction) and it was those old instincts, long buried, which came roaring back to life. At a glance, he could see the blood, and deduced that it was a bullet that had caused it. Charlotte, distraught, craned her head around to look at him, her eyes imploring. A passer-by – a young legal clerk named Penberry – came to their aid and, hot upon his heels, another pedestrian, this one a newspaper vendor, known to his friends and intimates as Charlie. Together these two gentlemen, following the careful directions of Mr Bentinck, and under the blurred and tearful gaze of Miss Charlotte, bore the recumbent Scrooge solemnly inside and laid him down in Mr Bentinck's office. The front of Mr Scrooge's shirt and jacket was now crimson and sodden. As soon as the poor man had been set down (and checked to ensure that his heart still beat and that he continued to draw breath), Secretary Bentinck ordered the young legal clerk to fetch a doctor as quickly as he could. "There's a man the Club uses, not far from here. Number 157 Sargasso Street. Ask for Dr Tarkin. There will be a sovereign in it for you if you run like the wind." "No need for that, sir," said Penberry. "I owe this gentleman a great deal. He once helped an aunt of mine – much-loved! – to rid herself of a spectre that wouldn't grant the poor old dame so much as a single night's sleep." Doubtless, had Mr Scrooge been conscious at this declaration, he would have remarked upon the role that coincidence (as in those two encounters which had already taken place that day) played in his life, and how the doing of a good deed for Mr Penberry's aunt some five or six years previously had now resulted in her nephew running from that makeshift sick room at a great pace and straining his every sinew to fetch a physician. Yet Mr Scrooge, though he breathed, did not know of anything that was taking place around him. His eyes were tight shut and he seemed altogether lost to the world. Once Mr Penberry had left, Charlotte turned to the secretary (and to that newspaper vendor who, finding himself now unable to help in any material form, stood rather awkwardly at the corner of the room, turning and turning his hat over in his hands). "Will he live, sir, do you think?" Though her tears had stopped now, her voice was strained and ragged. "I don't know," came Bentinck's honest reply. "I have no notion." "Then what can we do, sir?" she asked. Bentinck looked back at her, his expression a picture of helpless concern. "I think we should pray," said the newspaper seller, stepping forward, and putting his hands (and hat) behind his back. "I think we should pray for him, and for his old soul." And so, sinking to their knees in that little room, before the prone figure of Mr Scrooge, that is just what the three of them did.

## 一句话总结

第三十三章（Stave Three I）：What a scene there was played out upon the steps of The Club of Decent-Hearted Men! Mr Beck had fled back into the dreadful security of the city, leaving Ebenezer sprawled and wounded upon the threshold. Charlotte – that indefatigable maidservant – cradled the old man in her arms, calling for help. With commendable alacrity, the Club's secretary, Mr Bentinck, was out in the open air almost at once, to seek the reason for the commotion. Long ago, he had himself served his country in a military capacity (without, it must be said, a great deal of distinction) and it was those old instincts, long buried, which came roaring back to life. At a glance, he could see the blood, and deduced that it was a bullet that had caused it. Charlotte, distraught, craned her head around to look at him, her eyes imploring. A passer-by – a young legal clerk named Penberry – came to their aid and, hot upon his heels, another pedestrian, this one a newspaper vendor, known to his friends and intimates as Charlie. Together these two gentlemen, following the careful directions of Mr Bentinck, and under the blurred and tearful gaze of Miss Charlotte, bore the recumbent Scrooge solemnly inside and laid him down in Mr Bentinck's office. The front of Mr Scrooge's shirt and jacket was now crimson and sodden. As soon as the poor man had been set down (and checked to ensure that his heart still beat and that he continued to draw breath), Secretary Bentinck ordered the young legal clerk to fetch a doctor as quickly as he could. "There's a man the Club uses, not far from here. Number 157 Sargasso Street. Ask for Dr Tarkin. There will be a sovereign in it for you if you run like the wind." "No need for that, sir," said Penberry. "I owe this gentleman a great deal. He once helped an aunt of mine – much-loved! – to rid herself of a spectre that wouldn't grant the poor old dame so much as a single night's sleep." Doubtless, had Mr Scrooge been conscious at this declaration, he would have remarked upon the role that coincidence (as in those two encounters which had already taken place that day) played in his life, and how the doing of a good deed for Mr Penberry's aunt some five or six years previously had now resulted in her nephew running from that makeshift sick room at a great pace and straining his every sinew to fetch a physician. Yet Mr Scrooge, though he breathed, did not know of anything that was taking place around him. His eyes were tight shut and he seemed altogether lost to the world. Once Mr Penberry had left, Charlotte turned to the secretary (and to that newspaper vendor who, finding himself now unable to help in any material form, stood rather awkwardly at the corner of the room, turning and turning his hat over in his hands). "Will he live, sir, do you think?" Though her tears had stopped now, her voice was strained and ragged. "I don't know," came Bentinck's honest reply. "I have no notion." "Then what can we do, sir?" she asked. Bentinck looked back at her, his expression a picture of helpless concern. "I think we should pray," said the newspaper seller, stepping forward, and putting his hands (and hat) behind his back. "I think we should pray for him, and for his old soul."
