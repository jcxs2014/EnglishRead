# 22. IV

## 本章导航

**一句话概括**：Back on the train, now passing deeper into the east, past Colchester and on towards the distant sea; voice of military man low and gruff as he began to tell lady what she wished to know; beneath his words came dull unstinting repetition of wheels upon track; "In the time that I've been by the side of Mr Scrooge," he said, "I've met with – I should say – several hundred people who have come in answer to his advertisement. Of those, many were simply mistaken in their claims or else overwrought or too filled up with imagination. Some, I fear, were not entirely sane. A small number were liars, endeavouring to perpetuate a fraud."; "Why would they do that?" asked Mrs Fell; "Out to make a name for themselves," shrugged the captain. "And, after that, a profit no doubt. But Mr Scrooge isn't a man who cares to be made a fool of. They've not gulled him yet. For he's seen the real thing, you understand. He knows the world of spirits. And so he knows a forgery when he meets one."; "So tell me," pressed the lady, "about the real cases. The ones where they weren't just mad or lying or confused. Tell me about the one that you can't dismiss." There was a curious edge of hunger to her voice; "Are you sure?" asked Captain Ambrose. "I don't wish to unsettle you any further."; "Please. I should like to know. And it would ease my worries if I heard of a case in which you and Mr Scrooge had achieved some satisfactory result."; Captain Ambrose seemed to think for a moment, as if weighing up the wisdom of continuing. "There was a man who came to us last year. That was a difficult business, though in the end a happy one."; "Then that," said the lady decisively, "is what I should wish now to hear."; "It was in the summertime," said the captain, warming only gradually to his theme, for he was by no means a natural tale-teller, "and London was too hot. You know how it is? The prevailing mood of the place when the temperature of the city climbs high? The shortness of temper which afflicts the typical Londoner? The smells of the place?"; at this, the lady nodded; "Even The Club of Decent-Hearted Men was uncomfortable, even with the windows wide open and Mr Scrooge and I down to our shirtsleeves. Things had been quiet for us, as is often the way in our business during the summer months. My employer had been busying himself as best he could with numerous philanthropic endeavours, while I had the accounts to look through as well as other business affairs, for which the old man has rather lost his appetite. Yet there had been nothing of a supernatural nature for eight or nine weeks. And we found we rather missed it."; "Why do you say so?" asked the lady; the captain looked momentarily uncertain, perhaps at the plainness of the question. Then he said: "We like to help people, ma'am, of course, where we can." He paused. The rattle of the carriage. The grind of the wheels. "But I would be remiss if I did not also admit to you that there is, on occasion, a certain thrill to be had in the investigation of such matters."; "I see," said the lady, and her tone was not entirely approving; "My life needs some excitement, ma'am," said the captain. "I do not shrink from the admission."; "Excitement may take many different forms," said Mrs Fell softly. "But, please, do go on with your story."; "Well, it began as do most of the cases of Mr Scrooge. With a knock at the door of The Club for Decent-Hearted Men. And so it happened one hot morning last year that I answered the call to find a fellow standing on our threshold who looked as though he had fallen on hard times indeed. He was unkempt, ill-shaven and, in spite of the weather, dressed in a ragged, threadbare greatcoat. He looked at me with desperation. He seemed to rock and sway upon his heels as though he were in the grip of exhaustion. As I opened the door, he looked behind him, with an odd, frantic motion. Then he turned back to me. 'I need…' He sounded desperate. 'I need… Mr Scrooge.' And with that he toppled forward into a deep swoon. It was only by sheer instinct and luck that I was able to catch him in my arms before he collapsed to the ground."

A small number were liars, endeavouring to perpetuate a fraud."; "Why would they do that?" asked Mrs Fell; "Out to make a name for themselves," shrugged the captain. "And, after that, a profit no doubt. But Mr Scrooge isn't a man who cares to be made a fool of. They've not gulled him yet. For he's seen the real thing, you understand. He knows the world of spirits. And so he knows a forgery when he meets one."; "So tell me," pressed the lady, "about the real cases. The ones where they weren't just mad or lying or confused.
