# The American Berserk

Source: Granta
URL: https://granta.com/the-american-berserk/
Published: Thu, 10 Sep 2026 07:00:47 +0000

---

It’s a strange fate being an American. One of its small burdens is a national reality that notoriously outruns its fictional portrayals. Away from the country, I sometimes turn to that outmoded source of information, the nightly news on television. It begins with the latest natural disaster: wildfires, floods, tornadoes pulling roofs off houses, plywood and corrugated metal flying, and an interview with an ashen-faced pioneer: ‘We lost everything.’ Then, there is a story about local carnage. A man in the back of a police cruiser wriggles free of his handcuffs; the cop rushes around the car to detain him, only for the man to leap to the front seat, take the wheel and shift into high gear down the highway, until the cop, now in the backseat, tasers him and sends the car careening into a retaining wall. Next comes the White House Correspondent, reporting on foreign affairs: Clinton bombing a pharmaceutical factory in Sudan; Bush Jr revving up the Global War on Terror; Obama droning a Pakistani wedding party; Biden striking Syria; Trump obliterating a girls’ school in Iran. It ends with an upbeat segment. A beloved postman is fêted on the eve of his retirement. A baby deer is rescued from a drain.



What is the American Berserk? It’s there on the vomit-splattered decks of the Mayflower, and in John Winthrop’s terror that there might not even be enough wilderness in the New World. It’s the angel Moroni guiding Joseph Smith to unearth the golden plates that contain the ancient history of the continent. It’s John Brown loading his .52-caliber Beecher’s Bible. It’s 1st Platoon, C Company opening fire at Mỹ Lai. It’s Newt Gingrich demanding that giant mirrors direct the rays of the sun into American ghettos at night to prevent crime. It’s US forces invading Afghanistan, and setting up model Afghan villages in Louisiana where, in war games, Afghan refugees play themselves. Ancient Judea had the Virgin Birth, but California has the Octomom. The Berserk reaches us through the police body cam: dizzying, grainy, point-blank. It’s in the military hardware named after decimated Native American tribes: Apache, Black Hawk, Chinook. It’s Donald Trump presiding over an AI-generated advertisement for the ‘Gaza Riviera’. The American Berserk is characterized by world-historical excess. It is the mood of a country convinced of its chosenness but drenched in self-pity about that chosenness not working out.


We owe the phrase ‘the American Berserk’ to Philip Roth. It first appears in the opening section of American Pastoral. Set in the Vietnam years, the novel is about a thoroughly assimilated Jewish family in Newark, New Jersey. Swede Levov is an all-star athlete who inherits his father’s glove-making business and solidifies his standing in the upper middle class. But his daughter Merry rejects the life he provides. As a member of an antiwar terror cell, she blows up a local bank, killing four people. What drives her is unclear – her father may have overstepped in his affection for her when she was young; she may be a dupe of left-wing propaganda – but she becomes the enemy of everything that is wholesome in go-along-to-get-along America: its ‘angry, rebarbative spitting-out daughter’ who transports her father ‘out of the longed-for American pastoral and into everything that is its antithesis and its enemy, into the fury, the violence, and the desperation of the counterpastoral – into the indigenous American berserk’.


The American Berserk is what Saul Bellow, in a letter to Roth, described as ‘the purgation one can obtain only through rage’. It is a stringent will to clear away the flotsam of the country – to bulldoze accumulated corruptions – and pave the way for an idealized freedom. The ‘American Berserk’ is not a condition imported from elsewhere – Old Europe, Asiatic Bolshevism – in which the true country remains innocent. It comes from within the American experience; it is a force without direction, a desire without a clear objective. This is why it could change political coordinates even in Roth’s lifetime. First hurled against the extra-parliamentary Left, it now more firmly attaches to the rabble-rousers of the Right. Since 2016, headline after headline has warned of the resurrection of the berserk: donald trump is the authentic american berserk; how social media, the great recession and donald trump combined to bring out the indigenous american berserk. ‘If you’re not writing about the berserk, you’re not writing about America,’ chided the historian Rick Perlstein in 2020. ‘Is there a way for us to make the “American berserk” a little less . . . berserk?’ wondered the New York Times four years later. 


Though it seethes with contempt for ‘political correctness’, Roth’s late work was hardly proto-MAGA. But it was an extended paean to When America Was Great – when she was good. A firm nod in this direction comes early in American Pastoral when Roth, in the guise of his alter ego Nathan Zuckerman, lashes out against Leo Tolstoy’s The Death of Ivan Ilyich. The novella tells of a well-to-do judge living in comfort in St Petersburg, who begins to die a nasty death and realizes, at the last moment, that he lived his life in the wrong way. ‘Maybe in Russia in 1886,’ Zuckerman counters. ‘But in Old Rimrock, New Jersey, in 1995, when the Ivan Ilyiches come trooping back to lunch at the clubhouse after their morning round of golf and start to crow, “It doesn’t get any better than this,” they may be a lot closer to the truth than Leo Tolstoy ever was.’


In 1962, Edmund Wilson adopted the same view. ‘I don’t believe that a man like Ivan Ilyich could ever look back on his life and find it so empty and futile’, Wilson wrote in his diary. America’s greatest literary critic and one of its finest novelists together form part of a literary tradition that cannot admit the bitter wisdom of the Russian: that worldliness is doomed. The false idols of America – security, comfort, supremacy – have only been smashed by an alternative breed of writers who refuse to insist on the country’s inveterate decency.


 


America’s conflicted view of its mission has long been a source of unhappiness about its actions in the world. At the zenith of American power, in the 1940s and 1950s, there was little need for Washington to distinguish between husbanding global capitalism and America’s domestic well-being – they were very nearly the same thing. In the 1970s and 1980s, these two commitments diverged. It was no longer self-evident that the American working classes benefited from the world that the US’s political classes were shaping. Various insurgent candidates – Jesse Jackson, Ross Perot, Ralph Nader, Pat Buchanan, Bernie Sanders – challenged the status quo, but each was put in their place. The mission of the country – to maintain primacy in world affairs – remained consistent. But the cost of the empire, especially when it included building up rivals like Japan and West Germany, was retrospectively viewed as too high. When China entered the World Trade Organization in 2001, American elites could still convince themselves it was an ideological victory. It would not be considered one for long. Could the US continue to keep its primacy through trusted methods, or would it need a decisively new posture?


Donald Trump takes the second view, or at least pretends to. If his trolling about annexing Greenland or the Panama Canal shocked some, it effectively publicized that nothing was off the table for the new administration. Trump’s great attraction for Americans is not that he flatters them or harnesses their rage or reverses the tide of elite condescension. The reason Trump appeals is that, in a country where there exists no plausible opposition to the present political-economic order, and in a world where the US’s room for maneuver has been drastically curtailed, the figure who can best simulate agency is king.


For all his bombast, and for all his periodic baiting of American elites by flirting with tariffs, threatening to defund NATO, and mocking of the Supreme Court, Trump’s policies are for the most part conventional. Even his blundering into war against Iran was par for the course. Trump’s sheer unscriptedness, his sore winning, his stand-up antics, his ability to turn the electorate into rubberneckers watching him lick his own wounds: all of this keeps him at the center in a country where attention is the coin of the realm. When asked in 2017 if Trump, as the new emblem of the ‘American Berserk’, outstripped his imagination, Roth responded, ‘It isn’t Trump as a character, a human type – the real-estate type, the callow and callous killer capitalist – that outstrips the imagination. It is Trump as President of the United States.’


 


The writers for Granta’s American Berserk edition did not need instruction on what the berserk was. They nodded and got to work. Mary Gaitskill is the country’s greatest anatomist of the subterranean connections between pain and pleasure, and the national capacity for personal degradation. In her comic story ‘Intercession’, two new friends speak on the phone about the forms of punishing desire that crowd their aging lives, and the slinking sense that sanity is a chimera in today’s America.


It is hard to find a young American writer of reportage who does not look up to John Jeremiah Sullivan, one of its most abundantly talented practitioners. From the memoir of his sportswriter father, Blood Horses, to the essays on American music and television and literature, Sullivan writes with open wonder about his country. He belongs to the American line of writers that runs from the great old Southern masters through David Foster Wallace and Donald Antrim and, in his story for this issue, Ken Kesey. In ‘The Acute Wing’, which is set in a low-end psychiatric institution, a man confesses a terrible truth, and finds that, contrary to the cathartic promises of therapeutic culture, confession only amplifies the damage.


Christian Lorentzen once argued that the American literature of the Obama years could be categorized into four modes: autofiction; fables of meritocracy; stories of trauma, ‘in which suffering confers meaning in an otherwise comfortable world’; and historical novels that take place in the near past, ‘locating in recent decades the romantic grit and violence their nostalgic authors find lacking in the present’. What about the literature of the Trump era? The earliest fiction of the MAGA years was often marred by the sense that writers were trying to remain unsullied by a vulgar blip in history. Recent offerings are more farcically dystopian. Amie Barrodale has satirized America’s diseased judicial branch; here, in ‘Dathun’, she turns to the executive, in a story where a Trump-like figure serves as the vestigial mascot of a regime that has perfected its sadism. In Rebecca Curtis’s ‘Gold Can Stay’, a wife frets over her security fence in post-apocalyptic America, while her husband, ‘H-Bomb’, plots with a ‘world-famous sextillionaire neodymium magnate’ to end global poverty and restock the oceans by culling ‘the globe’s sapien-load by ninety-five percent’. Hansen Shi’s ‘Blood Boy’ transports us to San Francisco, where conniving young technologists trade dumplings and kinky sex to profit off the hottest AI deals. In Halle Hill’s ‘The Year of the Rat’, a Tennessee motel clerk seeks deliverance in conspiracy theories, hare-brained schemes and self-help videos. Kathryn Scanlan’s ‘Study for American Interior (Monday)’ is a feat of acoustic attention that transforms the sounds outside a house – cars, squirrels, construction – into strange poetry.


In ‘Hawaiian Fauna’, Tao Lin picks up a hitchhiking astrologist who is as conversant with extraterrestrials and planetary realignment as the author. Antonia Hitchens’s ‘Arc de Trump’ plunges into the underbelly of Washington, D.C.’s officials, lobbyists and influencers, and offers up a portrait of a court culture where Christian-maxxing and toadying are full-time jobs. For this issue, William T. Vollmann took to the streets of Havana, where the US energy blockade has further ravaged the people. With Ernest Hemingway, José Martí, and Tomás Gutiérrez Alea as his guides, Vollmann and his companions speak to Cubans from across the political spectrum about Trump and Marco Rubio’s lust for regime change.


In our poetry we feature Frederick Seidel, the great bard of the berserk, whose poems of sex and death create a rapturous, bouncing music. Patricia Lockwood uses the Reddit forum r/Nostalgia to write an oblique elegy for John Ashbery. In his deadpan poem ‘People Keep Finding Bodies in Joshua Tree’, Michael Robbins drives through the desert, listening for the voices of departed singers and friends. Maggie Millner finds the crushing beauty in unloved things: ‘A broadside printed COFFEE MAKES YOU QUEER, / McGovern button, shoebox of Hitachi Magic Wands’. In three poems from his series ‘Security Questions’, the Cuban poet Osdany Morales moves from questions asked of him by US Immigration and Customs to fragments of his own history.


In our photography, Ciarán Finlayson writes about the work of Zora J Murff, who reckons with the compromises and commitments of a black artist ascendant in the academy, while Seth Harp, lately subpoenaed by Congress for his exposé The Fort Bragg Cartel, writes about the weapons industry, captured in all its shiny banality by the Russian-born photographer Nikita Teryoshin.


Next up for Granta is an issue devoted to the literature of Nigeria, to be followed by issues on the Koreas and Mexico. There will also be a special issue called ‘Humiliation’, which could just as easily have been the title for this one.
