# The Acute Wing

Source: Granta
URL: https://granta.com/acute-wing/
Published: Thu, 10 Sep 2026 06:42:08 +0000

---

The man had been on the ward for only a couple of weeks when a nurse was fired, or at least reassigned, for talking to him inappropriately. A woman in her sixties, Julia, who was new. His first day had been one of her first, too. He noticed how she regarded him with a scrutiny that verged on scorn. When he asked her any question, even of the most straightforward kind, she would narrow her eyes and take a few drawn-out seconds to answer. It seemed he could all but hear her thoughts going, ‘Hmmmm . . . you.’ She was often on duty in the middle of the night, when he would stagger out to the desk looking for painkillers, the sadistically weak kind they offer when the system has flagged a history of pill addiction. He kept getting these headaches. Partly it had been the withdrawal, but they persisted beyond the point where that made sense as a cause. They would come on when he was sleeping. He woke to them in the dark, already whimpering. He had been prey to migraines since high school, and like all sufferers had evolved secret, eccentric techniques for surviving them, but these were new. The pain lay somewhere out beyond language, though he made efforts to describe it in the patient journal he’d been encouraged to keep. Like a poison dripped into the ear. Like a virus that had taken root in the muscles where the neck meets the skull and spread from there in a barbed network. The word ‘fire’ kept trying to insert itself, but fire is cleansing, and although these sensations might at times possess an illusion of burning, or of searing, they were equally a kind of cramp. Acid . . . cancer . . . the words slid down the surface of the thing, like the bloody hands of a stabbing victim down a diner window, and the failure of these attempts was somehow bound up with the pain. A strangeness of migraines, when you’re in the middle of one, is that, because they take place in the brain (or cranium), they often seem to be made of thoughts – the pain itself can resemble a form of consciousness, of sick thinking – which a person who doesn’t get them might assume would make it easier to articulate, but the opposite is true.


‘The etymology of the word “migraine”,’ he wrote in the journal – feeling, as he spelled the words, that he was appropriating, not quite convincingly, the voice of a man who no longer existed – ‘comes from the Greek hēmikrania, meaning half a head, which tracks, when you get them, because it seems like that’s what you’re doing, as you writhe around: searching for the half where it doesn’t live.’


One day a couple of neurologists showed up in his room. Two fit brown-haired white guys in white coats, in their thirties, they looked almost as alike as twins. Both seemed tired and leaned against the wall. One was the other’s assistant. The assistant glanced around a lot, as if scanning for exits, but the main doctor had a steady, exhausted gaze. Neither seemed pleased to be on the ward. He thought about how it had smelled to him at first, the urine and disinfectant, neglected bodily crevices, burnt-coffee breath, dead nicotine skin, despair pheromones. He couldn’t really smell it anymore, except at moments, when the doors of certain rooms and hallways flew open. The main neurologist let fall some vaguely patronizing sentences about how medical science had failed to determine what a migraine really was, and how some researchers suspected there might be a psychological dimension.


‘But they come on in my sleep,’ he said. ‘If it’s psychological, why would I get them when I’m not even conscious?’


The main neurologist shrugged and repeated his wisdom, that it was mysterious. Both doctors had a strange contempt in their eyes, although the contempt may have seemed strange to him only because he had encountered it so rarely in his life. He was an educated middle-to-upper-middle-class WASP man – conspicuously one, somehow, a sort of throwback, with his old-fashioned politeness – who had washed up at the Pines, the psychiatric wing of the local third-rate county hospital, a facility populated almost entirely by the indigent poor. The doctors and staff were not used to people like him there. People like him could pay to go to nicer, less harrowing places. But he was broke and had shitty insurance, if he still had insurance (they were working to verify that), and the night of his intake had been an emergency. He’d been mumbling to himself incessantly about God and scouring the house for a gun he believed to be there (in the end there was a shotgun in an upstairs closet, kept for snapping at intruders, but no ammunition), and so his wife of many years, who half wanted him dead by that point but was not mean enough to let it happen, loaded him into the back of the station wagon and physically deposited him in the lobby, like a giant foundling. And really he had felt like a baby, going in. A male nurse asked a series of simple questions, and he answered them guilelessly. They took his clothes, shoes, watch, everything, and wrapped him in a gown that felt like wearing paper towel, gave him fuzzy gray socks with grip-pads on the bottom, made him sit in a wheelchair – though he could walk, albeit unsteadily – and off he was rolled.


The first few nights he spent on the Level 1 ward, the acute wing, the lowest circle, a place for psychotics and recently attempted (or plausibly threatened) suicides. His roommate that night had been a pissed-off, grousing fat man with thick bandage-pads on his throat. He had a crusty tan like he’d been sitting by a motel pool his whole life. He kept saying how many times he had read the whole Bible (six), emphasizing that he read it page by page. ‘Most people don’t do that, see.’ It was never clear what these statements were meant to imply. That the man was spiritually superior? Or that he had read it all and found it unavailing? Later, when they had both been moved off the crisis-stabilization wing and wound up ‘in group’ together, and he heard the man repeat these claims to others, the point remained veiled. Was it a way of saying, ‘Alright, God, I read it! Are you gonna help me now?’ Finally one afternoon he’d asked the man, in front of everyone.


‘And what did you learn, from all of that?’


‘From the Bible?’ the man said, as if the question, despite its obviousness, had caught him unawares. ‘As far as I can tell, it’s totally empty.’


Those words hushed the room. Even the group leader had no response. A handful of the other patients seemed troubled, especially Gloria, a schizophrenic Black woman in her seventies who wore a plain denim dress and prayed under her breath all day. Now she squirmed in her chair and muttered.


‘Well, I don’t know!’ the fat man said, appearing to feel himself cryptically accused.


 


The incident with the nurse, Julia, happened right after that group meeting. He was sitting at one of the big wooden tables, watching a man named Dusty render precise and elaborate multicolored drawings with dull pencils. That was one of the main activities on the floor. Several of the patients on the ward were meth-heads, and this genre of hyper-fixated activity soothed their nervous systems. They would make pictures as complex and finely executed as Tibetan mandalas then cast them aside like scrap paper. The finished work held no interest, only the doing, although they were always dedicating their pieces to one another: ‘For Shasta, the most amazing person I have ever known. Love, Dusty.’ The drawings were all the more impressive because the pencils were never sharp. You had to take them up to the nurses’ station and ask for a sharpening, and the nurses knew how to grind them down just to the point where they could not be used to stab anything, which was wise, but less than ideal for coloring. The other problem was that the pencils themselves were small and stubby, about four inches long, like the little pencils they keep in baskets of fill-in menus at family restaurants. A person’s hand would start to ache almost immediately. Dusty showed him how to make a paper extension-cone and tape it onto the base of the pencil. That helped and was pretty much the only reason the man could maintain the journal.


He studied Dusty, mesmerized by the severity of his focus. A Bruce Willis movie played on the TV, high on the wall. The nurses played a lot of Bruce Willis movies, or movies like that, mainstream action. They were dulling. They kept to the surface. Dusty had his tongue between his teeth. He was small and skinny, with close-cropped hair. He had a beard like a teenager who couldn’t grow a real one yet, though he was not much younger than the man, early forties. He had crude, faded tattoos, but only a couple, and no white-supremacist stuff. The man liked Dusty. It seemed to him that Dusty had been kicked around a lot. Maybe Dusty had told him as much, when they’d first met. The man did not remember the first four or five days in the hospital very well. He mentioned it, the localized amnesia, to one of the nurses, who said it was normal.


Dusty finished his drawing – blue and pink, shards of fractals, a bird with an elaborate, fanned tail – and dedicated it to him: ‘For L—, great dude and my brother. Stay blessed, Dusty.’ The man took it to his room and put it on the little bedside table. He wanted to tack it to the sidewall, so he could look at it when lying on his side at night, but thumbtacks were out of the question. Maybe later he would ask for Scotch tape, or use Band-Aids. He sat down on the bed for a few seconds. He thought about how much the drawing meant to him – a lot, it seemed – and how odd it was that it meant so much. It hadn’t even meant much to Dusty, probably. The man retained enough of his mind to realize that. Still, it meant very much to him, and he knew that it shouldn’t, and this made him cry, but for just a few seconds. The tears dried right up, as if there had been only a few of them in his body. He lifted his eyes, and Dusty was standing just outside the door. ‘Smoke break, dawg,’ Dusty said.


To smoke cigarettes in those weeks was sensual in its intensity. His blood had not been that clean since his teenage years. There was nothing foreign in it now except two newer psychiatric drugs they were trying, and a modest amount of ADHD medication, to ease the abrupt withdrawal from a hardcore addiction to amphetamines (they didn’t tell him it was for the withdrawal; he guessed that they were sheepish about the off-label usage; at the same time he sensed, as was later confirmed, that they were pretty much constantly deploying it like this). The cigarettes made him sweat and calmed him at the same time, as if each were the first he had ever smoked and also one for which he had longed. His reflex was to describe the sensation as euphoric, but that word had intimations of joy in it, and there was no joy in his body. Feverish was the word. He sat there with the others on low concrete benches, in the ineffectual shade of some juvenile trees, and smiled at the crazy things everyone said. Shasta sat beside him. She seemed to like him, and he liked her. Their bodies were politely aware of each other. She was pretty, with brown wavy hair and thick eyebrows, but her face had been ravaged by the lifestyle – missing teeth, sun-blasted skin, and at times a sudden glaring look.


Dusty mentioned that, in some wards he’d been on, smoking wasn’t allowed, not even outside. They all shook their heads.


‘Why would you do that to people?’ the man said, and the others signaled agreement with wheezy laughter.


On their way back into the facility, Shasta fell into step with him, leaning close. ‘Would you do something for me?’ she whispered.


‘Of course,’ the man said.


‘When they give you the Adderall this afternoon, try to keep it in your mouth. Don’t swallow it. And give it to me?’


‘Oh, jeez,’ he said. ‘I don’t know if I can.’ He pictured the scenario: the nurse who gave him the pills would stand there until she was confident he had swallowed them. ‘Doesn’t it dissolve pretty fast?’


‘I don’t care if it’s half-dissolved,’ she said. ‘We do it all the time.’


The man said he would try but that he wasn’t sure. Shasta looked disappointed. But her mind fluttered on to other thoughts, and she scooted away.


Inside they sat down again at the big table, but almost instantly the others stood up. They ‘had group’, but the man was not in this group. Each person on the ward had a different schedule. The schedules were written fresh each morning on a dry-erase board that hung on the wall directly below the television.


The man rose and went to his room, where he grabbed his journal and cone-extended pencil. He brought them back to the table and started to write. He was alone. He had been writing for maybe five minutes when the nurse, Julia, walked up to the table. Already that was strange. As a rule, the nurses did not venture forth from behind the desk area. They had a protected zone back there. In an emergency they might run out onto the floor, but it was irregular. Her body language seemed off too. She glanced around a couple of times.


The man stopped writing and looked up. ‘Hi,’ he said. He assumed that she had come to ask him about the migraines. Even though she was never especially warm or solicitous when he stumbled out asking for medicine in the night, he figured she must have some humanity in her, else why become a nurse of this kind?


‘I’ve noticed you’re always writing,’ she said.


‘Yeah,’ he said. ‘It’s part of my therapy. I’m supposed to keep this journal.’


‘Hmm,’ she said, and slowly nodded. The nod was skeptical. Displeased, even, as if there had been something false in his answer.


‘You’ve been having an affair, right?’ she said. ‘I read it in your file.’


The shock and mortification hit him at the same time. His face flushed. ‘That’s true,’ he said.


‘Well, don’t you think you should stop?’


He smiled a smile that was meant to express amazement at her audacity, but he even as it entered his face he could feel it betraying other feelings, ones that he would have preferred to conceal.


‘It’s complicated,’ he said.


‘Is it?’ She didn’t sit, just stood bending over the table, with her palms flat on it. ‘See, I’ve been through that. In my own life.’


A surge of indignation went through him. What was this?


‘How long have you been married?’


‘A very long time,’ he said.


‘Well, wouldn’t you want to just honor that?’


He muttered something pitifully vague, and she said, now a little sharply, ‘But those are just your feelings.’


Then she walked away, and he sat there, slapped. He tried to get back to writing. It was useless. Julia was stewing behind the desk. He could follow her shape in his peripheral vision, as he stared slack-jawed at the TV. A man was running. He wasn’t Bruce Willis, but a version of him. Strong, bald, somber, ugly-handsome. After some minutes had passed, Julia re-approached.


‘I’m sorry about that,’ she said. ‘I think maybe I . . .’


‘That’s okay,’ he said, smiling blankly.


He had meant it as a kindness. He felt for her, despite his anger. Or a part of him felt for a part of her, for some quality of woundedness that he noticed in her bearing. But she took the brevity and flatness of his response – along with, it was true, something peremptory in the timing – as a rebuff, and walked away. He felt guilty then, though abstractly aware that he’d been within his rights. This had been a thing with the man for as long as he could remember. He was ashamed of his own self-defense mechanism. He perceived it as a mean, unworthy instinct. At the base of that shame lay a sort of hypertrophied empathy. His conscience was always whispering that other people were every bit as real, registered injuries just as deeply, were twisting no less helplessly in the sea-trash fishing nets of human nature. You experience people as stronger than you, above you (said the voice), in those moments when life’s dissolving equations hand them a bit more power, but like you they are losing swiftly and inexorably to death and driven mad by the prospect. How dare you hit them, even under the guise of hitting back? Yet this very habit of forbearance, he more and more strongly suspected, had its roots in brokenness, not authentic mercy. It did no one any good.


He probably would have been content to let the thing with nurse Julia go – or not content, but would have stuck with his habit of quietly stitching the incident into a blanket of troubling memories he pulled over his head at night – but the following morning he made the mistake, if mistake it was, of telling his assigned therapist, Amanda, and Amanda became very angry, as pissed off as he had ever seen a therapist get, and the man had seen many therapists over the span of more than twenty-five years. He was what they called a special case, when it came to therapy, on account of things that had happened to him as a child.


He grew up in rural Oregon, in the northwestern part of the state, in a village called Fern Falls, about twenty miles from the coast. It was a chilly, bosky world of modest frame houses and trailers half-hidden at the ends of gravel roads. The windows were always wet and fogged. Everything had lichens on it. In his freshman year of high school, it had rained more in that town than anywhere else in North America. This was the thing he told people when they asked what it was like there. As factoids of personal trivia go, it was quick and good, both anecdotal and synecdochal. ‘What’s it like? Well, you know, it’s the rainiest place in America . . .’ That actually was kind of interesting. But he never said anything else about the town. If the conversation moved anywhere from there, it was to rain in the abstract. For all his acquaintances knew, he’d been raised in a palace of rain, living on droplets of mist like those creatures that have evolved in the spray-zones of waterfalls (hygropetric was the word – he had looked it up multiple times, could never quite remember it, or else misremembered the first half of it as hydro, which means water, of course, whereas hygro has to do with moisture). Yet there had once been a solid hometown. The man remembered it better than any place he had lived since. He could summon the texture, humidity-slickened, against his palm, of every erratic boulder of the place; the mottled amber of morning sunlight on damp cedar bark; the greasy pasted-in newspaper liner at the bottoms of kitchen drawers, and the printed words legible there, their meaning truncated by arbitrary folds and corners, yet given some weird incantatory power by how frequently they were seen, unconsciously memorized (‘they do it all quietly and with grace / I’ve never enjoyed any work / in the beautiful milk roaring 20’s television’): the human-like gasps that rose in the throats of a few specific pipes just before the notes caught in the organ at the Lutheran church; and the brook that ran behind the house, at the bottom of a hill, with a chain-link fence alongside to keep kids from drowning, and how one night there was a flood, and after the water receded he wandered down in the morning, a toddler, and found that a fish, in the hours when the fence had been submerged, had swum into one of the metal diamonds, got stuck, and now hung in the air, flying dead, and how his parents had not believed him when he tried to describe the sight, or had not understood his language, he was that small, so he led them by their hands down to the fence and pointed.


He kept all of that at the center of a complicated maze.


No. The man scratched out some words with the cone-pencil. From the outside his gestures looked furious, panicked, but inwardly they were driven by sheer meticulousness. Those pencils had no erasers. He wasn’t sure why. They’d been chopped flat on the butt ends. He tried to imagine how a person might use an eraser to commit suicide. Ah, right – it was the small metal cylinder that kept it affixed to the wood. You could flatten that out . . .


No, ‘all of that’, those sensory details, they were the maze. It had assembled itself from those fragments. One whole wall of the maze were these flecks of orange thread, seen from extremely close up, in the oatmeal-colored carpet in the basement of that house. One room was a cluster of pop songs that had streamed through the radio vents of his father’s beige Volkswagen Beetle in 1981: ‘Lady’ by Kenny Rogers, ‘I Love a Rainy Night’ by Eddie Rabbitt, ‘Queen of Hearts’ by Juice Newton. One sharp left turn in the maze was a sentence, pertaining to him, that he had half heard, when he was twelve, through a closed bathroom door, and spent about a decade deconstructing and reconstructing from the available syllables and silences.


He could make his way to the center, where the what-happened was, when he wanted. And did pay regular visits. It was crucial to stay in contact with it. He feared little more than becoming one of those poor souls suspected of having invented their ‘recovered’ trauma, so he never allowed it to get covered. To others, to the outside world it remained as secret as the coordinates of an undetected star, but for his part he tended it like an old burial plot, from those days when custom frowned on letting grass grow over the grave. The experience was always hovering, even as it occupied a distinct reality. It turned out this was a survivable way to be, or had seemed so for about forty years. Now some bough had snapped and left him here.


He stared at the lined paper of the journal.


Could it be that there was no longer any reason not to speak plainly?


 


He had been born into one of those families who adopt foster children for a living. He was the only biological child in the household, which meant that for most of his early life people assumed that he too was a foster, to the extent that he stopped correcting them. Very few of the people in Fern Falls could have told you which he was. He grew up with kids of various ages passing through the house. Many of them had behavioral issues, and he had been introduced way too young to a lot of advanced sexual stuff.


Counterintuitively, perhaps, it was not a foster child who had molested him, but a great-uncle – the brother, to be precise, of his maternal grandmother – a man who was a good deal younger than his grandmother, and in fact barely older than his mother, and thus presented to the boy more like a regular uncle, or some other adult relative in his mother’s generation. The great-uncle had appeared in Fern Falls one afternoon with a tweed suitcase and talk of a ‘rough patch’ in his life, and they took him in without hesitation, offering him the newly finished basement bedroom. Unusually, for that household, only one foster was staying there at the time, a girl about four. The man – that is, the boy – had turned six in that year. The great-uncle was fortyish. Slender, trim. Vitalis and engineer’s glasses. He dressed in a modest, elegant way. He had a reedy voice and chose his words precisely – two at a time, it often seemed. He had strong forearms covered in wiry black hair.


The ‘series of incidents’ occurred during a period of about six weeks in the winter of 1980. The younger girl was there each time. A film was made. It continues to circulate in that darkest of undergrounds, where it is known to aficionados as ‘Blazer Boy’ or ‘blazrboy’. He had never watched the film, and learned of its existence only in the mid-nineties when a federal agent, an Interpol liaison at the FBI, had contacted him at his college to inform him that his name and some personal data had been posted in a Central European chatroom. The agency had disappeared (‘digitally expunged’) the post, but the agent wished him to understand that an indeterminate number of people out there had seen it. The agent suspected that the poster had been the other adult in the film (there is another man, briefly visible, though his face is never seen), but ‘the investigation’ had ‘failed to make headway on that’. The man did not remember a fourth person having been in the room on those mornings, only his great-uncle and the girl. Somehow he had edited out the other adult. He related this to the agent, Russ, a genial, overweight, gray-bearded cyber-security expert, on the night they met, at a chain steakhouse off the highway in East Portland, and for most of the rest of his life – twenty more years and counting – had been replaying the agent’s response, ‘Well, he was there.’


He did remember the camera. Remembered it well, because it was the first video camera that he had ever seen in person. A tripod, cords. The great-uncle had been ‘into electronics’.


A decade after that first meeting, Russ contacted him again, this time via email, which by then had come into popular use. Russ had kept polite tabs on him, knew that he had become a writer, and asked him to produce a victim-impact statement – not on the abuse itself, in any graphic sense, but on the psychic effects of learning about the film and knowing that it was out there, on the Internet. Russ explained that such a document could help to secure more appropriate sentences in cases similar to his. Naturally the man had agreed, and lost sleep in an effort to craft something ‘impactful’. Russ assured him that it was good, and sent him very sporadic notes over the years to let him know that the statement had been introduced at a particular trial or used as a teaching tool at a conference. But those messages stopped after a while.


In truth, the man had felt, from the beginning, a certain remoteness from what he had written. The statement was a lie. Not in the sense that the abuse had never occurred, but because the abuse – what had taken place in the basement – had not been the worst of it, or not the totality of it. He couldn’t really tell anyone about the rest, not when his father had still been alive, although he had picked up a faint indication that Russ already possessed some inkling. It had been the tone in the agent’s voice when remarking on the fact that the great-uncle had died right around the time the abuse had taken place. ‘That’s interesting,’ Russ had said flatly, and the man had gripped his own knee for a second and released it, and there had been a pause in the flow of their talk, and in that pause the agent had noticed the young man’s choosing to let it go, or his not rising to address the observation. The agent had nodded and moved on. The whole non-exchange took maybe two seconds but turned, even as it was happening, into a micro-chapter of the man’s life, a hallway of the maze.


What had happened was this. One afternoon, a few days after the last of those three mornings in the basement, the boy had committed a petty naughtiness. Something banal. He had hurt the foster girl. They’d been playing, he’d pushed her, and she had hit her head on the edge of the stone fireplace. She wasn’t hurt badly, but it bled, and she cried. His mother stood above him, scolding him, next to the algae-filmed glass of the fish tank in the den. She held his wrist and demanded to know why he had done it. He had no explanation.


In place of one, he tattled and told her about what had been going on in the basement. He could not, as an adult, remember exactly what he, the boy, had said, what words he had used, except for one sentence, ‘He made us!’


There had been no calculation in his confession. It was pure deflection. He had hurt the girl, and his mother was displeased with him, so he would tell his mother about something worse that had been done to him – to him and to the girl – and that would make it all cease to matter. They would forget like they always did. Still, the boy must have understood that what had happened in the basement was bad. But he had no way of knowing how bad. The experience itself had been more confusing than painful. At moments there had been pleasure.


It must have been a weekend day, because the boy’s father was home. The father was in the kitchen at that moment, probably stirring instant coffee (the only thing the boy had ever seen him do in the kitchen), and he overheard the boy.


His mother’s features assumed a stricken O, and she looked away. He would never know how to describe it, not that he had ever tried to describe it to anyone but himself. The moment of her own death might be the only other time in her life when she would wear that expression, if the death were violent and sudden but left an instant for some reaction.


She put her hands on his ears, but the boy could already hear the muffled pounding of his father’s footsteps on the stairs to the basement. He had never heard his father move that fast.


He heard a shrill screaming, like an old woman might have emitted. It happened in two bursts, the second louder than the first. His memory pinched out after that. There was the sound of the ambulance, picked up through the walls of his room; a visit from the police, days later, during which he was told to go outside and play, and did, but hovered at the edge of the neighbor’s yard and spied, and watched the two cops, both older men, say goodbye to his father and walk to their car; and finally the sudden deepening of the silence between his parents, and changes in their manner toward him, marked in his mother by a bewildering rage, born of resentment, that she herself was distressed by, and for which she sought to compensate with unfamiliar, desperate displays of affection, and in his father by a kind of chipper formality, accompanied by a new and disconcerting habit of calling him ‘buddy’. The atmosphere in the house never re-stabilized. Everything got quieter – in part because his parents stopped taking in fosters. The little girl had been gone within days, back into the system, and then all of that was over. His parents took an endless series of promising jobs. Childhood passed.


One of his many therapists, Dr Pfeiffer – early fifties, second career, twice married with no kids or grown kids, ‘burnt out’ by the ‘corporate world’, with soft, curly gray hair, a moustache and sweater, cords and moccasin-like shoes, everything about him furred and fuzz-bearing: you wanted to roll a lint-roller down him – had said to the man, midway through their ‘work together’, that possibly it had ‘stabilized his ego’ to know that his father had been willing to commit violence to protect him, that in an admittedly terrible way it may have been the best thing that ever happened to him.


The therapist after that one, Monica, a Jewish woman in her late forties with long black-dyed hair and protruding eyes and New Age tendencies (boots and skirts, bangles along her forearms) had looked at him, after hearing that, and said, with a signature lack of affect, ‘You were raped, on at least three occasions, at the age of six. You listened to your father beat your uncle to death, and felt that on some level it was because of something you’d done.’


‘My great-uncle,’ the man said.


‘Right,’ she said. ‘And since that day, not a single word has been spoken about those events, in your family?’


The man had been silent then.


‘I highly, highly doubt that that stabilized anything.’


Then she had asked him to hold two paddles, one in each hand. She delivered an electrical charge into them, using a device that she held in her lap. She made one paddle buzz and then the other, or both at the same time. Oscillating. He gripped them tight, as if in hopes of squeezing out whatever mysterious power they contained. He was hunched over and sweaty. His anxiety had reached a level where it was hard to speak. Was it really possible that this shit helped people? Finally he sputtered, ‘This is freaking me out!’ and she turned off the machine.


‘I’m sorry,’ she said, as she gathered the bundle of paddles and wires up out of his lap.


Every therapist had his or her own pet method. Monica had the paddles. The old bespectacled Yankee transplant in Mississippi, the memory of whose name he had left down there, had rubbed his hands together and said, ‘Ah, another creative! I love my creatives,’ told him to meditate, and prescribed him a generous daily dose of amphetamines. Fuzzy Pfeiffer had been into making his patients do an exercise in which two chairs were placed facing each other. The man was encouraged to move back and forth between the two seats, assuming in each position the role of one side or the other in whatever internal debate underlay the torment. What had been his argument with himself, in those days? It seemed incredible that he could hardly remember . . . some cluster of thought-sensations that he had learned to call depression or anxiety or obsessive-compulsive disorder or most often a hybrid term, whatever the discourse favored in that particular half-decade. Each shrink would prescribe him the antidepressants du moment, and he would gamely try them, eventually re-arriving at a plateau of functionality, which he dubbed ‘better’, while continuing to feel wretched, until after a few years the pain grew intense enough again to trigger a reinitiation of the cycle, and all the while the reasons, the damage, festered down there in the literal and psychic basement. Looked at that way, it made sense that he couldn’t remember what the two-chair argument with himself had been, or even what bogus description of the problem he had offered, because he was never telling the truth about his interior world. She reminded him that she had seen him do something similar, in group. There had been a day when one of the bandage-necked men, the gaunt gray one, had confessed to the others the terrible thing he had done, as a young man, that had led him to attempt taking what he called ‘the coward’s way out’. He described a night more than fifty years ago when he had abandoned his family, just up and done the actual ‘I’m going out to get cigarettes’ number, leaving behind a wife and two sons. He drove west, to the desert, and made a new life. He described the pain that this rash decision had wrought on his family – wife had remarried a horrible man; son had committed suicide; daughter was miserable – as well as his lifelong confusion about why he’d done it. ‘I just couldn’t . . . I felt that I would die . . .’ At one point he had tried to reconnect, but the experience had only compounded his despair. They barely knew who he was. ‘I’m angry that this didn’t work,’ he said, pointing to the bandage on his neck. It needed to be changed. It had begun to yellow at the edges. The others listened with what appeared to be a combination of horror and contempt. Amanda did not interrupt.


Finally the gaunt gray fellow had run out of words, and Amanda had turned to the group. The man sensed that no one else was going to speak, to address the old man’s confession, not even his friend and fellow bandaged man. The silence was so unacceptable. In all the gaunt gray man’s seventy-five years of pseudo-life, this was doubtless as close as he had come – or, it was clear, would ever come – to baring his soul, and everyone in the room just stared at him.


The man set his coffee down on the floor between his feet. He looked earnestly at the man as he spoke. ‘You have to forgive yourself,’ he said. And: ‘There is not a single person in this room with you right now who hasn’t done things they regret every day.’ And: ‘The fact that you’ve been torturing yourself about this for so many years – half your life! – proves you have a conscience.’ The old man was listening. The others were mm-hmming. Amanda watched with an expression somewhere between pleasure and scrutiny, or one that seemed to contain a measure of both.


‘Are you a priest?’ the old man said. His tone was not quite placeable. It caught the man off guard and made him go quiet. He had the same reaction now, listening to Amanda. She was working to bolster his self-esteem. He took this as a sign that she had failed to detect the hidden narcissism in him. This belief, that she had ever failed to detect it, was itself the saddest and most unmistakable sign of that narcissism, but he wasn’t there yet.


‘Didn’t that feel good, when Glen said you’d helped him?’


‘You mean when he asked me if I was a priest?’


The man asked that with an edge of skepticism but Amanda seemed taken aback. That wasn’t how she remembered it, maybe, or how she’d heard it.


‘Sorry,’ the man said. ‘It’s just, when he said that, I heard him saying basically, “Where do you get off?” I mean, I’m one of the most fucked up people in that room. Why am I giving advice?’


She held her smile, but her eyes narrowed quizzically, as though the man had given her something to think about. He couldn’t read her thoughts.


‘I tell you what,’ she said. ‘It’s almost two, so we have to stop, but I want you to write something about this, for tomorrow. In your journal. Will you do that? Help me understand it?’


In the quiet hour after dinner, everyone was on the floor, but no one paid attention to him. He sat down at a table, slid a new pencil into the paper cone, and smoothed down the tape that held it in.


 


On the last plane trip I took before I ended up here, I sat next to a young flight attendant. She wasn’t working the flight – she was ‘deadheading’, flying as a regular passenger. She was watching a movie on her laptop, I don’t know the title. A man has a son who becomes a heroin addict. They’re fighting to save him. Toward the end of the flight and the end of the film, I looked at the side of her face, and she was sobbing so hard that she couldn’t breathe right. She had her uniform on and everything. She was about twenty-six, I think? Blonde with a round pale face. I tapped her on the arm and asked if she was okay. She pulled out her earbuds and said that she was okay but that she shouldn’t have watched the movie. It came too close to her own life. She had a lot of addiction close to her, she said. I asked what she meant and she said that both of her parents were heroin addicts. She was flying into Atlanta because her father had just gotten out of detox there. Her parents were both alive, but divorced, and both heavily addicted, and she said that every night she went to sleep wondering if she’d get ‘the call’ about one of them. She had two brothers, but they were teenagers, too young to get it or be of any help. She worried about them, that they’d start using, or just how they’d been scarred. They lived with her grandparents. She had moved to Salt Lake City, a place she had no connection to, just to have some kind of life, to feel separate enough from her parents’ drama that she could breathe. But she thought about them constantly. ‘It’s been my whole life,’ she said. I told her about my father. Said I knew what it was like to watch self-destructive people kill themselves in slow motion, and to love them but not be able to save them. To want greater closeness but need greater distance. I couldn’t believe how much pain was in her face. She was too young for that level of pain. I said I’d pray for her parents. I wanted to laugh at myself then, but it had seemed inhuman not to say it. She said, ‘I can’t believe I’m having this conversation. You’re a total stranger.’ I said, ‘I know, but it’s okay, because I get it.’ But I don’t know if I did get it, or if I do. And it made me think about myself, this strange ability I have, at moments like that, to empty myself. I could talk to anyone, somebody dying in a field, someone running from the law, and really listen. It’s easy. It’s gallows kindness. I tell myself that people blow through me like fog. But then, do they really? I mean, here I am thinking about her story, and writing about it, and I probably will pray for her parents. But I can’t shake the feeling that a person more real than I am, or more present, would not have been able or wanted to have that conversation in the first place, would instead have respected the distance. When we were walking off the plane I said, ‘Take care of yourself,’ and she said, ‘Thank you,’ and her face was so serious when she said it, so far beyond any politeness. Then you walk off the plane, and you’re in the airport, with people streaming by . . .


 


The following morning, the man sat in his chair and watched Amanda reading this passage in his patient journal. He didn’t want to stare at her but couldn’t stop glancing at her face. It was full of skeptical attention.


She gave the journal back to the man, then settled back into her office chair and regarded him.


‘I need to think about that,’ she said, with a wary laugh.


‘Totally fair,’ he said.


‘What do you think about it?’


‘Well, I . . . like, if it were written by someone else? I would probably think, “Get over yourself.” ’


She cocked her head to the side. ‘Say more.’


‘Why does he even care about all that shit? The woman was suffering. He made her feel better. That’s what matters. The fact that he’s so caught up in his role in their interaction, whether it was pure, whether it’s . . . I don’t know . . .’


‘If you look at the literature,’ she said, ‘the psychiatric literature, when you’re talking about chronic self-deprecation, once you unwind it, there’s usually shame at the core. Unresolved trauma.’


‘“Self-deprecation” sounds so mild,’ he said. ‘This is more of almost, like, a phantom feeling. Vampiric or something . . .’


It seemed she took that seriously.


He got the feeling that she enjoyed working with him. The other patients’ problems . . . it wasn’t that they were less serious. The opposite: they were so serious that it was more like social work. Whereas he was giving her some of the old-line bourgeois Freudian family sexual psychosis, the kind of thing she had studied in college. He flattered himself there. Probably lots of the other patients had life experiences like his, but their damage manifested differently, having been compounded by subsequent violence. In any case, he was not wholly wrong about Amanda.


‘Tell me what else,’ she said. ‘Where are you with things? Have you been talking with . . .’ She named his wife and the other woman.


That’s when he made the mistake. He told Amanda what the nurse, Julia, had done, how she had approached him on the floor, and reproached him.


‘I won’t lie,’ he said. ‘It kind of fucked me up.’


Had he been in his right mind, or closer to it, he might have thought about the impact these words could have on Julia and her livelihood. As it was, the idea that he had the power to affect the destiny of somebody who worked at the Pines never occurred to him. He had thought that it was like complaining about a cop. Nor was he clear on the fact that Amanda ranked significantly above Julia in the hospital hierarchy. It should have been clear but wasn’t. He saw, by the kindling of a black fire in her eyes, that he had triggered consequences. It was the only time in their many sessions, one-on-one and group, that he witnessed her losing composure. She had to hold the arms of her chair to keep from . . . he didn’t know what. Storming off down the hall to the desk to cause a scene. But she quickly regained control, sharply crossing her legs and breathing through her nose.


‘I am extremely upset to hear that,’ she said.


He felt the old panic. And knew exactly the moment it went back to. She saw it, too.


He stammered. ‘I’m sure she was trying to do the right thing. To help me or whatever. But I guess it kind of freaked me out that she had read my file. Or that she would confront me with it?’


Amanda opened her mouth to speak but thought twice and said nothing. He guessed that she had pulled back from addressing the matter of the file. The nurses were not supposed to have access to the therapy notes, and they sure as hell weren’t allowed to discuss them with patients. It raised a host of issues, partly legal. Mainly, though, she seemed concerned about the psychological harm it could have done, or had already done, to the man. She felt he was making progress toward a mode of existence not centered on shame.


‘I’ll have to say something about this,’ she said, in a measured tone.


The man sighed, squirmed in his chair, straightened himself, put his hands between his knees. ‘Please don’t do that,’ he said. ‘I really don’t want anything to happen to her.’


‘I’m afraid I don’t have a choice.’


‘Really? But if I’m asking you –’


‘Listen,’ she said, leaning forward. ‘You don’t have to feel bad. You’re not making the decision, okay? We have a weekly meeting, all the nurses and doctors and staff, and the whole point is to bring up stuff like this. I can’t just . . .’


They both, at the same moment, became aware of the same awkwardness. It consisted in how she was talking to him: not as a patient. She wasn’t exactly talking to him as an equal or a colleague, either, but something too close to that. It was clear she felt beholden to explain and apologize. They both felt her authority dim. It was terrible, actually. They both needed her authority. Their dynamic, so precious and hard-won, had been destabilized.


 


That night the man suffered what Amanda later described in his file as ‘a significant setback’.


It was brought on by his doing something stupid, a thing he knew that Amanda, and any sane person, would have cautioned against. He logged on to the Internet. They had a computer on the ward, tucked into a corner of the floor, at a blond-wood desk with high sides, like an old library carrel. Hardly anybody touched the machine. The hospital had it set up – deliberately, one had to assume – so that it functioned only with agonizing slowness. But you could type in your gmail or hotmail or whatever and then sit there for ten minutes while the homepage loaded, and then enter your username and password and hit enter and sit there for another ten minutes while the next page loaded, then click the individual message you wanted to read – five minutes. It was pointless, but it allowed the Pines to say that the patients had access to email.


The man had woken up that night with a migraine so bad it scared him. He thought it might be something else, like an aneurysm. He flung his body out to the desk, muttering apologies to Dwayne Chavis as he cracked the door. The nurses, so accustomed by then to watching him lurch out of his room in the middle of the night to ask for pain pills that they had mentally folded it into the ritual of their shift, did not overreact. They gave him the usual medicine and let him sit in one of the big chairs.


One of the many reasons it was stupid of him to look at the computer was that it strained his eyes, potentially worsening the migraine. But then, this is another facet of cluster-migraine pain: in the midst of one, any sensation is preferable, even other pain. People stab themselves, prick themselves with needles, smash themselves with hammers, literally beat their faces against walls.


So he checked his email. He had his right hand on top of his head, and with his thumb and fingers he aggressively massaged his temples. He glanced over at the nurses. They were watching him. Julia was absent. Normally she would have been there. He didn’t know if this had anything to do with his earlier conversation with Amanda. Maybe this was just her night off. But something felt off. Their looks seemed less friendly. This disturbed him. What if Julia had been right? What if she were some kind of angelic being, the only person telling him the truth, and now he had engineered her banishment. Without meaning to, sure. But was that even true? He was so full of shit, after all.


The screen loaded. It showed a long list of his unopened emails, with their truncated subject headings alongside off to the right. He read down through, looking for personal messages embedded amid the crap. Because of the migraine, which had begun to fade but only barely, he had to keep glancing away. To the nurses it must have looked as if the screen were physically burning his eyes, as if he were jerking his gaze away like you’d snatch your hand from a scalding tap. He knew he looked insane, in other words. There were a few messages from friends: ‘Just checking in, man. Sounds like things are intense on the home front.’ Some of his friends weren’t speaking to him, but a few were, and had written, and it was meaningful. At the same time he was pretty sure that if they knew more about him and what he had been up to, they would have cut him off, too, and this sense of a fraudulently earned concern made him shrink from allowing himself the solace.


There were two bad messages. The first was from his mother, with whom he had not spoken in about five years. It concerned his uncle Patrick, his father’s younger brother. His father had been dead for more than twenty years. The man had assumed, or would have assumed if he had given it any thought, that the brother was dead, too, but it turned out that he was still alive, albeit barely. He had tried to kill himself again. That was what his mother was writing to tell him, that his uncle had attempted suicide for the third time. She did not specify what method he had used, only that he had come near enough to success to put himself into a coma. They expected that he would be pulled off of life support soon, but that hadn’t happened yet. She just wanted the man to know, in case he ‘felt like praying for his uncle’. He did. He tried. He did it out loud, which was weird, with the nurses watching. That was probably the beginning of the episode. As he prayed, he thought about his lanky uncle, that ghost-like whiskered Irish face, prematurely white hair that ran in the family. He replayed for the thousandth time the most vividly remembered and disturbing of his childhood visits to see the uncle, who lived in Portland. His father dropped him off at the apartment and drove into the city for some interview. He was probably eleven? Uncle Patrick sat cross-legged on the floor in front of the boy, who was on the couch, and rolled up a joint. They sat there in silence as the uncle inhaled several times. The boy had nothing else to look at. The uncle was a person who made a point of having no TV. He worked as a substitute teacher. He said it suited him. He would let them know when he was available to work, and the rest of the time . . .


‘Ask me a question,’ the uncle said, when he was nice and baked. ‘Any question.’


The boy thought for a few seconds. There wasn’t anything he wanted to know from the uncle. ‘What happens to you after you die?’ he said.


‘What happens to you?’


‘Yeah, do you go on being your same self? Is there, like, a heaven, or . . .’


‘In Buddhism,’ the uncle said, ‘there’s a term, Mu. Have you heard of that?’


‘Like moot? I’ve heard of moot.’


‘No, this is Mu. Basically it means that the seriousness of the question is not equal to the seriousness of the answer.’


‘Oh,’ the boy said.


‘Your question is Mu.’


The boy’s face reddened.


‘Your question is also moot,’ the uncle said, and laughed so hard he fell sideways.


Now the uncle was almost gone. They let him finish dying four days later.


What most troubled the man about the suicide attempt, however, was not the sad childhood memories it conjured but a different story involving his father and uncle, one that his father had told him shortly before he died. It had to do with the uncle’s first psychotic break, which had happened when he was about twenty, sometime in the late sixties. The man’s father had received a call that his little brother needed to be committed. His father had raced over to find him in the midst of a full-on raving episode. He was ranting about their father, who had died fairly young, in his fifties. Once, when the uncle was a boy, his leg had been badly broken in a football game, and after the bone was set at the hospital, the grandfather, whom they all worshipped, had carried him out in his arms. This was one of the uncle’s most powerful childhood memories, waking up and looking into his own father’s face as they had moved down the hallway, through the doors and into the light. Now the uncle, in his madness, had become convinced that because his own father was dead, there would be no way for him ever to leave the hospital. This time he would be trapped there forever. The man’s father had been forced to knock him unconscious.


This memory sent the man’s mind plunging. And it seemed impossible that the timing of it all could be a coincidence: Here he was, in the hospital, in the psych ward, and his uncle, who had killed himself, almost, was also in the hospital? His uncle was trying to send him a message, to warn him that he would never get out.


The other message was from the woman. He had spotted it instantly, when the list was loading, and froze. Told himself not to click on it. Now he clicked, and waited. A picture started to load. He looked back at the nurses. They were busy at tasks, or telling each other about their kids. He closed his eyes and tried to breathe well. Opened them again.


Her new film was having a premiere at a festival in London, and she wanted him to see the posters for it. She had worked with the organizers to create a limited series of hand-painted posters. They hung in the window of a theater, or maybe it was a restaurant.


There were words on the poster.


The words were words he had written to her. A moony sentence about what survives in the dark. She had turned it into some kind of advertisement.


The man remembered little of what followed, except that he was shouting. Or they were telling someone not to shout. The others had come out onto the floor. He had woken them. Dusty, Shasta, Dwayne, and Gloria, all just stood and stared. Shasta had her hand on her mouth. Was she laughing? A male nurse he had never seen before was approaching with one hand forward.


 


They drugged him, and he slept without a migraine for the first time in many weeks, and dreamed hard, and in his dreams he re-entered the maze.


One section, a corridor that he hadn’t gone down in forever, was lit with fluorescent bulbs that sounded like they had flies buzzing in them. It was the name of a third-grade teacher’s assistant, Miss Arlington, a tall and graceful young woman, just out of college, with brown hair that she wore in a bun every day. She was ‘doing her training’ in his class and must have noticed something, because she put in a request with the principal that she be allowed to visit the boy’s home. She had shown up at the door one day on a rainy spring afternoon. She and his mother stood talking in the entryway, and the boy was sent to his room, but he had lingered in the hallway, trying to listen. Miss Arlington was not invited any further into the house – very irregular, socially – and his mother never mentioned the visit or what had been discussed. Nor did the boy ever say anything. He could have asked Miss Arlington, when he saw her at school the following day, ‘Why did you come to my house?’


 


He woke in the room where he had spent that first night, with the horrible fat man. They had transferred him back to the acute wing. His wife was there.


He had woken up in hospital rooms to find her with him before, and she had always been holding his hand, or resting hers on his leg. This time, she had placed her chair a few feet away from his bed. He had lost her.
