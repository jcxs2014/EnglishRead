I was half dead in the Jacksonville airport. I was exhausted and they had a group of middle schoolers up in the bleachers singing Christmas carols. I was keeping my head down. I had my sunglasses on, and I wasn’t trying to cause trouble. Honest to God, I was mostly thinking about the parking garage. I was thinking about the fact that I had not operated a motor vehicle in about seven years and I had the fear deep in me about getting the car out of that parking garage.


I was alone driving from the East Coast of Florida to Joshua Tree, in California. That was a normal thing to do after a breakup. My boyfriend had just dumped me. I wasn’t surprised. We kept having the same fight. But after I’d landed, successfully made it out of the airport parking garage, and driven about sixty miles, I pulled into a gas station in Lake City and started pacing in the light rain. The breakup was inconvenient because we had planned to take the trip together. It would have been romantic. Camping in a minor national park. Eating barbecue on the side of a county highway. At the very least, it would have been useful to have him around. My ex-boyfriend had opinions about women operating heavy machinery, and he could’ve driven the car.


In the gas station I drank a Celsius. Then I ate a turkey sandwich, with sweet pickles and mayonnaise. I chugged half a bottle of water. I took off my pants and changed into a pair of pink shorts, made of some kind of canvas material. And I got back in the car. I wasn’t going to back out. I was writing a piece about teenage girls in America, driving across the country to interview them, and I had already found the girls I would talk to. Almost all of them were the children of friends of friends, because when you suggest to a complete stranger that you want to hang out with their teen daughter and just chat, people assume things. I explained that I wanted to ask the girls what they liked to do, where they hung out. This seemed like a good enough place to start. I wanted to see how their lives mapped onto my own experiences of being a teenager, to see what, if anything, had changed.

Biloxi is a place I always remembered because I like the name. Mobile is a place I’ve always pronounced the wrong way. I would say MoebIsle. You’re supposed to pronounce it like this: MoeBeel. Someone tells me Biloxi is the Vegas of the Gulf Coast. Cecily tells me Mobile is a pretty weird place to grow up. But she’s not quite from Mobile. She’s from Spanish Fort.


Cecily is seventeen years old. Three years older than Al, at an age where three years feels like twenty. She is the girlfriend of my friend’s much younger little brother. Spanish Fort, she tells me, has only really suburbanized in the past decade or so. It was very Rural before. Cecily doesn’t live there anymore. Things are bad with her parents. Her father kicked her out. Cecily’s mother is a self-proclaimed witch and, Cecily tells me, has gotten arrested for taking all her clothes off in the street.


Cecily goes to a prestigious public art high school in Birmingham, Alabama. This is where she lives. The school has dorms, for boarders, since it covers the whole state of Alabama, not just Birmingham and its suburbs. Cecily wants to be a famous writer. I asked her to write her life story.

By Cecily Andromeda Turner



I am a young essayist born in the Holy Gardens of Swampy South Alabama. I realised that I could not stop asking the girls about love and if they thought it was real.


Unlike them, I was never in love with anyone as a teenage girl. But I was in love with the idea of being in love.


I didn’t actually fall in love for the first time until I moved to Paris. I knew it was love because I felt different. I wanted to tell this to the girls. Not any of the details. But the part where it took me a long time. I wanted to tell them that I didn’t fall in love until I was nearly twenty-one, but that over the course of my twenties I would fall in love four other times. That each time it happened I became a different person, that it was scary, that it wasn’t always good, that sometimes I still felt exactly the same as I had done as a teenager.
