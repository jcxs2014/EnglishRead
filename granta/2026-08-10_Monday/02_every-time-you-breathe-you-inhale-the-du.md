# Every Time You Breathe, You Inhale the Dust from Our Bones

Source: Granta
URL: https://granta.com/every-time-you-breathe-you-inhale-the-dust-from-our-bones/
Published: Tue, 07 Jul 2026 15:11:10 +0000

---

Seated at the desk in the airy living room, Johanne squeezes her eyes shut in concentration. One by one she transfers numbers from the invoices onto a pad of gridded squares. She blows eraser debris off the paper, working slowly and attentively. A single incorrectly copied number can determine whether the accounts balance out. And the worst, Johanne knows, is when the accounts don’t balance out, the humiliation of unpicking the seams.


She jiggles the pencil between two fingers. The accounts have become increasingly complicated. It’s a good sign, she reminds herself, a sign of growth. She gathers her hands around the warm mug of coffee; the steam pearls on her forehead. She takes a gulp and grimaces. Agh! she exclaims, setting down the mug. The sound of footsteps by the door.


‘Kresten, is that you?’


Then Kresten is standing in the doorway, winded by the stairs. The years have turned him plump and portly, a little tumefy and ruddy in the cheeks. But even Kresten was a tiny baby once, lived inside a belly, Johanne thinks, tempering her response to the unexpected interruption.


‘Keys,’ he says in a huff.


Johanne points at the nail where the keys to the truck are hanging.


‘Right,’ he says, and drops the keys into his pocket.


Whenever a problem is solved, or he finds something that was missing, that’s what he says: right. And every time he says it, a twig is added to the fire inside Johanne that hasn’t yet been ignited. She doesn’t know what kind of fire the kindling is for. But she feels that something is building up.


‘Did someone call?’


‘What?’


‘I thought I heard you from the stairs.’


‘It was just the coffee.’


Kresten looks confused.


‘I burned myself,’ Johanne explains, sticking out her tongue.


Kresten nods, irritated by this useless piece of information. He grabs the doorknob to leave. This man that once made Johanne tremble with desire. A dryness in her mouth every time she saw him, a sudden desert, and Kresten was the source.


‘Bye now!’ she shouts after him.


‘If you get the chance,’ he says, sticking his head in from the hall again, ‘some klejner would be nice.’


‘Bye now!’ Johanne repeats, a little sharper to shoo him out, to mark a boundary. She doesn’t appreciate that kind of request.


 


The front door slams behind Kresten. Johanne pokes her burned tongue out of her mouth again. Poul, she thinks, smiling at the thought of how her son would laugh if he had been home and seen her sitting here with the accounts and her tongue hanging out of her mouth like a dog. Mom, he would say, if you had a tail you’d be wagging it. He teases her and says she loves the accounts. And she really does. The way the numbers make sense and can make everything resolve. She both dreads and looks forward to the day Poul will take over. But she can still follow along with him, she says encouragingly to herself, entering a few more expenses in a column. She hasn’t yet been overtaken by second-grade math. But it’s only a matter of time. With each passing year, Poul is growing larger and smarter than her. But for now, Johanne is still holding the pen, making two horizontal lines beneath the total expenses and income. And there wouldn’t be a business in the first place if it wasn’t for her. Kresten could acknowledge that a little more.


She blows on her coffee and takes a careful sip. Burned child, she thinks, smiling to herself. She’s in good spirits. The holiday is right around the corner. She and Poul have cut out Christmas hearts and made endless paper garlands. Kresten has been busy and barely home. One night, after he fell asleep in his chair, Poul hung the gold-glittering garlands from his ears. It was wonderful, it was a peek into another life. The person Kresen was also capable of being. She and Poul giggled like naughty children, bursting into laughter when Kresten awoke in confusion. It took a moment for him to understand what they were laughing at, dopey and bashful with the garlands hanging from his ears, letting himself be made fun of. We’re a real family, Johanne thought, and she felt something warm and bouncy spread through her body, a feeling she would hereafter call the garlands.


 


But anyway. Kresten could acknowledge her a little more. Lately, she had considered saying, Kresten, I would like my own money. Everything I do for the business, and good honest work deserves to be paid. That kind of logic resonates with Kresten. But she also knows how infuriated he would be, hearing her say that – haven’t I always given you all you’ve asked for, have you ever been lacking for anything? His self-righteousness, just beneath the sugared surface.


She presses her palm firmly against the hole puncher. Puts four holes into the sheet of paper and lets yet another invoice slide into the blue binder. In the corner in capital letters, she’s written PAID.


The thought of her own money. Money that wasn’t for household costs, money that was hers alone, little doses of freedom. What couldn’t she do! What would she do? Order a dress from the catalog? Spend a little on the children, Poul and Finn. She and Valborg could go on holiday, just the two of them, now that the boys are older, stay for a night or two, spend a whole afternoon being waited on.


How much do you need for that, Kresten would ask with a mix of incredulity and resignation. It’s not so much about that, Johanne would respond, it’s more that you don’t see my work as deserving of a salary.


 


The bricklayers treat her with respect. But it’s mostly, she suspects, because of the summer party she throws each year. When she pulls out all the stops. She spends days frying fish and boiling potatoes, making pea soup and aspic, a leg of lamb with caramelized rhubarb. Once a year, the local guesthouse pales in comparison. If the weather is agreeable, the party is in the yard, with candles and croquet on the grass. She takes care of everything. All of the bricklayers and their wives are invited. Just like the accounts, the party grows each year, as more and more of them are hired. Kresten’s employees mainly associate Johanne with the party – the generous and beautiful hostess – and not the accounts that she takes care of every day. And when people don’t pay their dues, she has to call them up. It’s no pleasant task – what hasn’t she been called over the years. Close-fisted, petty and worse. Kresten would never make those calls, he’s too good-natured, jovial, and friends with everyone. He’s happiest with a trowel and a cold beer in his hand. Sitting on an upturned cement bucket, having a well-deserved break. No, Johanne is standing in the wings, turning the wheels.


 


Johanne flips through the binder, forcefully. A page tears, right down the middle. She fills her chest with air, holds it in, while she mends the torn page with tape.


He’s been lucky, Kresten, Johanne thinks, letting out the air in her chest, he’s hit a gold mine. Houses are being built like never before, more and more people can afford to buy their own. It’s a good time to be a bricklayer, making people’s dreams come true: cookie-cutter houses, simple, rectangular rooms, where life can play out in its calm and quiet way.


They’ve been lucky, she corrects herself. Kresten and she. They’re a pair, an entity, even though their love has become tamer over the years. It’s been a long time since he turned her mouth into a desert, a long time since he’s been able to quench her thirst.


Johanne runs a finger over the repaired sheet of paper.


She’s heard them talking about the economy on the radio. The upswing, they say in stern voices, making it sound like a gigantic seesaw they’re all sitting on. The drop in the stomach, getting thrown into the air, and the body lifting from the seat, as if it’s being catapulted into the universe and releasing its connection to the earth entirely. They don’t mention the seesaw on the radio, but that’s the feeling it awakens in Johanne. A shared leap, long and happy, forward, onwards, up.


 


Now the telephone is ringing. A shrill and ecstatic jingle fills the room. Johanne rises and grabs the receiver. Hansen residence. She nods. Set to start in May. Yes, of course. I’ll pass that on. Good good. Carefully she places the receiver back in its cradle and makes a few notes on the pad. Johanne holds a great respect for the telephone. Ever since it arrived in their home, she’s had a sense of coming closer to the world. The thought of the cables running along the seafloor across the Atlantic makes her nerves quiver, the transatlantic connections, telephone conversations and voices swooshing through the copper pipes many kilometers beneath the sea’s surface. She nuzzles the cord between her fingers; the whole world is there, like a possibility in her hand. The American president is at the end of this cord, and Mamie Eisenhower – are they really as happy as they look in the photographs, sitting out on the lawn in staged chairs in front of their house? Mamie in the dress patterned with large flowers, the three-strands of pearls. In one of the pictures Johanne has seen, Eisenhower looks like he’s sulking.


Across the street, two cranky seagulls are fighting over something on the ground, a preserved peach, a tough piece of meat? Johanne’s eyes flicker against the unruly light. The pupils that have become so accustomed to the December darkness contract, turning into small, sharp pen tips in the sunlight.


She turns her gaze back to the living room, pupils opening against the dark mahogany, the green canvas folded tightly around the backrest gives Johanne the sense of being swaddled, held in place by a beautiful piece of upholstery.


Over the back of the chair, a necktie hangs like a pennant in still air. Johanne takes the fabric between her fingers, strokes the smooth silk. She pulls the necktie from the back of the chair and up around her collarbone, ties it around her throat. How many times has she tied a knot around Kresten’s neck, but never around her own. She laughs a little at herself, her sudden impulses, that it’s never occurred to her before to try on a tie. She takes a turn around the living room, challenging the tie. Calm and resolute, she navigates the furniture, considering each piece: there is a chair, a feather-shaped bowl, a brass standing ashtray. The tie hangs from her neck like a long, dark-blue tongue, her face takes on a discreet twist at the corner of the mouth, a slightly raised eyebrow. Johanne throws up a hand with spread fingers, an explanatory gesture to convince a crowd. A palm sweeping the living room. Sweeping every doubt out of the way and lifting the air – the entire atmosphere – up. It’s a gesture full of history, tradition, an expression of authority. One must bend to the dust. One must stay close to this power.


There’s a quiver in her stomach, a feeling of wellbeing, potency, of the ability to create one’s own circumstance. Eisenhower sulking on his sun porch, like every man that has had history on one side and a beautiful woman on the other.


Now then, Johanne says to herself and the imagined crowd. She turns on her heel and walks with a bluff countenance to the desk to put the final touches on the accounts.


She adds a few columns, the red pen for expenses and the green for income. Clears her throat and adjusts the tie, it’s so blissful, first to write and then to add up all the numbers. Collecting all twelve months to see how it turns out. The balanced accounts. It looks promising, Johanne can already tell. It’s shaping up to be yet another good year.
