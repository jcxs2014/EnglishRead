# The Other Norwegian

Source: Granta
URL: https://granta.com/the-other-norwegian/
Published: Tue, 30 Jun 2026 13:33:00 +0000

---

Jon Fosse, whose work I’ve been translating for twenty-five years now, is not only a writer – he is a language activist. He writes in Nynorsk, the so-called minority Norwegian language, and has championed Nynorsk his whole career. He was involved in founding the second National Theater of Norway, which performs plays in Nynorsk, including his own, and he has also translated modern classics into Nynorsk – his version of James Joyce’s ‘The Dead’ is advertised as ‘the first translation of Joyce into Nynorsk’, who up until then had only been translated into the majority Norwegian language, Bokmål. When Fosse won the Nobel Prize for Literature in 2023, he was often described in the press as a writer of a ‘minority language’, but just what that meant was often obscure to anyone outside of Norway.


There are typical minority languages in Norway, spoken by the indigenous Sámi and harshly suppressed in Norway since the 1850s though recognized now, or else spoken by immigrants – all mutually unintelligible with Norwegian – but Nynorsk is not that kind of minority language. Nynorsk and Bokmål are both ‘Norwegian’. They are used by roughly 10–15 and 80–85 percent of Norwegian speakers, respectively. They are spelled slightly differently but are mutually intelligible, with almost identical grammar and vocabulary. The case of Norway is unlike that of multilingual countries with actually different languages: Belgium’s French and Dutch, Canada’s English and French, India’s twenty-two officially recognized languages. A good analogy to Bokmål and Nynorsk might be Northern and Southern US English, if Southlish had an official spelling system, dictionary, academy, and language activists. But it doesn’t, which is why Americans speak with Northern or Southern ‘accents’. So what is a minority language that is nearly the same as the majority language? What is Nynorsk?


Norway was ruled by Denmark for four centuries until it received independence, though it was forced to accept a union with Sweden under a common monarch. Under Denmark’s rule, Norway’s language of government, law and education was Danish, sometimes spoken with an accent; the 1814 constitution was written in something its authors called ‘Norwegian’, but which was in fact Danish. Then, in Norway’s new nineteenth-century era of relative freedom, two nationalist language movements emerged at once. The business, literary and political elite declared, ‘We don’t speak Dano-Norwegian, it’s Norwegio-Danish!’ and pushed linguistic reforms to bring written Norwegian closer to the spoken, less Danish-influenced forms, at least as spoken in Oslo. They gave it a new, more nationalist name as well: Riksmål, or the ‘National Language’. At the same time, Ivar Aasen, a farmer’s son and a great figure of post-Enlightenment Romanticism, walked the length and breadth of the country to collect dialects from the countryside and reconstruct what Old Norse should have developed into, and would have, if it weren’t for Danish interference. He focused on the grammar and pronunciation of the rural dialects of western Norway and the inland east, deemphasizing city dialects and the southeastern coasts as ‘too corrupted by Danish’. He called the resulting composite language he came up with Landsmål, the ‘Language of the Country’: his version of the National Language. Importantly, Aasen didn’t pick a favorite dialect and base the language on that, but set out to find or create a common denominator among all the rural dialects. Spoken Norwegian varied, as did the national languages in many other European countries at that time. Italy was a not-yet-nation of dialects and ‘Frenchmen’ were famously mutually unintelligible from one village to the next, with French a foreign language for half the country’s citizens in the 1870s. Aasen aspired to synthesize the spoken forms, and it is a matter of perspective whether you say he ‘invented’ Landsmål/Nynorsk or ‘discovered’ it. Either way, he single-handedly wrote and published the definitive grammar and then dictionary of Landsmål, and was the first to write literature in it – prose as well as poems that are still beloved Norwegian songs today. Over the following decades other writers began writing in Landsmål too, and soon the language acquired literary prestige.


A public debate on whether Landsmål ought to exist at all took place in 1852, and further legislative battles ensued, alongside polemics in the press over which of the two strategies would result in a true Norwegian: modifying Danish into a language for Norwegians, or adopting this independent alternative to the colonial language. Neither side was trying to change how people spoke, only how they wrote it down, and each side saw themselves as nationalist champions of Norway against Denmark specifically; Aasen once said he would have never bothered to do what he did if Sweden had owned them instead of Denmark, because Swedish sounded closer to Norwegian, as it still does today (written Norwegian looks a lot like Danish; spoken Norwegian sounds a lot like Swedish).


Both Riksmål and Landsmål gained official status in 1885 and Norway gained full independence from Sweden in 1905, before either form of the language fully won out, so both are still legally mandated. A series of reforms to Riksmål over the following two decades further distinguished it from Danish (or Dano-Norwegian), to the point that they became clearly separate languages that required translation. Henrik Ibsen wrote in this pre-reformed language and his Norwegian is what we’d now have to call Danish – indeed, Denmark includes Ibsen in its national canon. His plays have to be translated into Norwegian, although it is called ‘translation’ if it is into Nynorsk, and ‘updating’ or ‘modifying’ if it is into Bokmål. In 1929, Riksmål was renamed Bokmål (‘the [normal] Written Language’, i.e. the one in the books) and Landsmål became Nynorsk, a name always mistranslated into English as ‘New Norwegian’ despite the fact that it seems to be based on older forms of the language. It would be more correct to translate Nynorsk as ‘Modern Norse’: the (reconstructed) successor to Old Norse, in the same way Old English and Middle English led to Modern English – not to ‘New English’ – or Old French to Modern French. But unfortunately no one calls it that.


In Norway today official documents have to be produced in both languages; schools have to teach both, though local councils decide which one is primary in each school district and parish; government speeches can be given in either. Nynorsk has an official status that Catalan or Provençal can only dream of. And yet Bokmål and Nynorsk are almost exactly the same, at least if you’re only reading them. Nynorsk spells ‘not’ as ikkje, Bokmål as ikke – and so what, when I read either one I understand it to mean ‘not’ and move on. Nynorsk infinitives traditionally end in –a; Bokmål infinitives end in –e. ‘Church’ is kyrkje or kirke. It seems ridiculous that every government document needs to be printed and promulgated in duplicate.


In the mid-twentieth century, the Norwegian Parliament said enough is enough and created a language council to agree on one national language, Samnorsk (‘Common Norwegian’), but the –a infinitive people wouldn’t give up their –a’s, the –e infinitive people wouldn’t give up their –e’s. Contemporary Norwegian language activists have emerged in defense of each language, and each has a radical wing (which is actually conservative) and a conservative wing (which is actually radical); the radical wings each advocate for intervention to bring the opposing language closer to the other, and the conservative wings push to conserve what makes their language distinct by adopting an older form of it – Høgnorsk or ‘High Norwegian’ for Nynorsk, and pre-1938 Riksmål for Bokmål.


Meanwhile it’s not entirely clear whether they’re even languages at all. Technically they are both ‘written standards’ – spelling systems – or at least they used to be: a 2022 law officially designated them ‘languages,’ while a 2024 proposal to change the constitution, to be voted on sometime before 2028, took a step backwards and called them ‘written standards’ again. Either way, there is no official spoken Norwegian language: everyone is free to use their own dialect, of which there are still dozens. In practice, Bokmål closely matches the main Oslo dialect (Urban East Norwegian), while Nynorsk is close to some of the western dialects, including Fosse’s own. Other dialects sound their own way. And yet, as of now, the Wikipedia pages for both Nynorsk and Bokmål give the number of native speakers as ‘None (written only).’


When Fitzcarraldo started publishing my translations of Fosse in 2018, the editors and I discussed whether to label the book as ‘translated from the Nynorsk’, or ‘the New Norwegian’, or ‘the Norwegian (Nynorsk)’, but any of those choices would have interfered with metadata and Library of Congress cataloging, and put Fosse’s book into a different language category than other Norwegian books, including those written in uncredited Nynorsk. Officially, then, Fosse’s English books are translated ‘from the Norwegian’.


 


 


While the difference between Nynorsk and Bokmål barely registers in English, it is more meaningful than there simply being two spelling systems. An international context makes it easier to see some of the ways in which the overall rhythms of the two Norwegian languages differ. Bokmål is closer to Danish and thus to both a written tradition and German; Nynorsk is closer to English. (The twelfth-century Norwegian kings, speaking Old Norse, could understand and be understood by the English.) You can see it in the spelling – the English freedom, German Freiheit, is fridom in Nynorsk, frihet in Bokmål – but it runs deeper. Bokmål, like German, tends to express things as nouns; Nynorsk, like good writing in English, prefers vivid verbs. Victoria Kielland, a Norwegian writer I translate from Bokmål, produces effects of Faulknerian overheatedness and a crippling lack of psychological boundaries by using impersonal phrases, passive sentences and unclear subjects. In one scene, to translate literally, ‘a fear rose up in the throat’, but whose? It could either be the victim’s or the killer’s. I used to think that this was just her style, but I now recognize that this kind of translation problem is the same as the ones I know from translating German into English. They are not present in translating Fosse, and this may be due to his language, not just to his individual style. In Norway, law students are sometimes encouraged to write first drafts of assignments in Nynorsk and then translate themselves into Bokmål, the primary language of legal documents. This way they bypass the turgid and convoluted qualities of Bokmål, and achieve a clarity and orality that makes the documents readable and understandable.


Thinking of Bokmål as more written and Nynorsk as more spoken can be misleading, though. By being based on multiple dialects, Nynorsk is conceptually more distant from the spoken language than Bokmål is from its singular source: Oslo speech. A colleague of Fosse’s flat-out refused to ‘say something in Nynorsk’ for me: she said she speaks in her dialect, and she can speak Bokmål, but she can only write in Nynorsk and then read it ‘in a Nynorsk way’. Fosse disagreed, telling me that she speaks ‘very like normalized Nynorsk’ and in her speeches actually uses a lot of Bokmål. Meanwhile Ottar Grepstad, perhaps the leading Nynorsk advocate in the country (unless you count Fosse) who has written dozens of books on the language, from the belletristic to the statistical, including a well-received popular biography of Aasen, insists that Nynorsk is a language like any other, both written and spoken – the Norwegian dialects are the spoken forms of Nynorsk; it’s just that Nynorsk doesn’t have a single spoken standard.


When speakers use a dialect that doesn’t exactly match either written standard, they generally plump for Bokmål over Nynorsk, especially if they’ve moved from a rural area to a city. Bokmål is used by most media, advertising and publishing, and therefore seems default. Nynorsk, as a result, relies on protection and government intervention. But while Bokmål may seem like the urban elite language with Nynorsk the language of the countryside, in practice the people who care about writing in Nynorsk, or performing plays in Nynorsk, tend to be professors at universities, or polemicists in prestige publications. ‘No one actually speaks Nynorsk except broadcasters,’ is something one hears in Norway, while Grepstad and others with statistics on their side point out that that’s not actually true. Fosse himself occupies both oppositional positions: a man of the people standing up for his own authentic language, as well as a representative of the literary language with its own great tradition (Tarjei Vesaas, Olav H. Hauge), used by a disproportionate number of Norwegian writers today.


Is Nynorsk still a marker of authentic, non-Danified Norwegianness, as it had been? Håkon Kolmannskog, the publisher of Samlaget – founded in 1868 as an activist organization to get Nynorsk books published, and now the major Nynorsk publishing house, including of Fosse’s books – grew up in the Bokmål camp, but became a self-described ‘convert’ to Nynorsk in the 1990s. This linguistic conversion was a way to affiliate with his Norwegian heritage and happened in tandem with becoming a Marxist and campaigning ‘No’ in the 1994 referendum on Norway joining the EU. For him, Nynorsk was a badge of political identity (though not family background). Unlike Kolmannskog, Peder Lofnes Hauge, the somewhat younger head of Noregs Mållag (Norwegian Language League), the pro-Nynorsk organization, became an activist for more personal reasons: after growing up in a majority-Nynorsk town and school, he moved to Bergen, found himself in the minority, and was politicized to fight for his rights and preferences. I asked Fosse if he felt Nynorsk was ‘more Norwegian’ than Bokmål, and he said yes, in a historical sense, but today you can’t say that – 80 percent of the Norwegian population live within two hours of Oslofjord and thus basically speak Bokmål.


The more involved I got in the languages, the more I was reminded that language use is embedded in personal and political history: you just can’t drop down into the culture in 2026 and ask what the difference between ikke and ikkje means. Your choice of Bokmål or Nynorsk is a form of self-expression, and how you pronounce ikke or ikkje signals who you are, who you want to be. But how could government speeches even be given in Bokmål or Nynorsk, or plays be put on in Nynorsk or Bokmål, if these are just spelling systems with an emotional and political charge? A listener can’t see how the lines of a speech or play are spelled. What does it mean to ‘speak’ these languages at all?


The reason I cared was because of what Nynorsk meant to Fosse. In his Nobel Prize acceptance speech, which I translated as A Silent Language, he spoke about how he learned as a child that he ‘had to take [his] language back . . . and had to do it on [his] own terms.’ His recent novel Vaim doesn’t mention Nynorsk and Bokmål per se but is deeply concerned with the urban/rural culture clash in Norway that the language conflict partly maps onto. Nynorsk is far more than spelling rules for Fosse, it’s the language he grew up with and part of his identity. In essays he’s said he can no more argue for its existence than justify his own. He grew up speaking a dialect close to written Nynorsk and told me, ‘I can hear my grandmother say something to me in Nynorsk, I cannot hear her say something in Bokmål.’ As a child he learned to say stein for ‘stone’, not the softer sten, and these memories of language-learning are connected to the mood of what he’s writing; when he went back to the –a infinitives he had learned growing up, from the less purist Nynorsk he had adopted as a young writer when almost all the writers he admired used –e’s, he said he felt like his writing changed a lot! He laughed at himself when he said it, but he meant it.


Fosse has said that Nynorsk’s status as a lesser-used language has left it freer from degradation by advertising or political uses, and thus let it remain more poetic and human. But he also told me that he thinks his situation is comparable to Kafka’s, whose Yiddish- and Czech-inflected German operated in contrast to dominant forms and let him create a ‘minor literature’. Like Kafka’s German, or Beckett or Synge’s Irish English, Fosse using the ‘minor language’ within Norwegian gives his writing a different literary power. But this analogy implies that Nynorsk is just an outsider form within Norwegian, not a language of its own. Which is it? I asked him, and he told me, ‘Both – it depends on the conversation you’re having.’
