I have a key to the flat, but I haven’t been since.


Does anyone else have one? I don’t think so. Who would? Gyuszi’s mum is dead, and his dad left when he and his brother Peti were children. He doesn’t keep in touch with his father and Peti lives in America. Peti couldn’t travel back yet although he said he would try to get home for the funeral somehow. I was to handle everything until then. Peti gave his consent for the cremation. He wanted to send money but I told him it wasn’t necessary, Gyuszi had taken care of everything in advance. He had calculated exactly how much the cremation would cost and what else would need to be paid for. That’s what he was like. I have all the money here in my bank account.


His friends kept calling one after the other and sent messages of commiseration. Everyone treated me like I was his widow though we weren’t married. In the twelve years we never even moved in together. I had a husband before, Gyuszi had a wife, and neither of us wanted to start the whole rigmarole again. Although . . . that’s not true. At the very beginning, in the year after we met, I would have liked to have got closer to him. When we were first together, I wanted to see him every day. I was in love.


But that was a long time ago. That was when I came up to Budapest and I imagined one day we’d have a house together and maybe a child. That was when I could still have kids, I was only thirty-eight. Gyuszi hadn’t had a child with his wife either. When I brought up the idea, he mostly shrugged his shoulders. We’re fine as we are, he said, what do we need kids for? And then time passed and eventually we couldn’t.


To tell the truth I don’t mind. I look after my nieces a lot, and in the summer they stay with me for two weeks.


They’re called Alma and Réka. There are two years between them; one is fourteen, the other sixteen. Now they’re really getting to be teenagers and they’re a lot more difficult than they were before. My sister is happy if they come for a weekend and she can be alone a while. They’re forever tapping at those screens. I can barely get them to put away their phones for lunch.


But Gyuszi could always convince them to go out and cycle with him. I can’t even get them into the garden. They huddle in a dark room and message each other on their phones. That can’t be normal, can it?


Gyuszi never wanted to live together. He preferred us moving back and forth. He often slept at mine but didn’t bring his things. He just kept a pair of pyjamas there and a few T-shirts. We spent the bigger holidays together but after a few days he always went home. He said he missed his own brain and that he needed to be alone. That he’s a lone wolf, which I should respect. Over the years I came to terms with it: that’s just how he is. Keeps himself to himself, likes to retreat to his cave. Sometimes he would go into hiding for weeks. We’d speak on the phone but he wouldn’t come over. If I asked him to do something though, he did it. The gutter, the sink, odd jobs.


I went to his workplace too. He ran an enormous warehouse on some kind of lot. When he drove me once, it was weird to think that for years he’d been travelling there, every single day. We passed a forest, then, beyond the roundabout, were large industrial warehouses, and at the end, almost in the middle of nowhere, there stood this grey, vacant structure. We parked, the rain drizzled, at a box on the wall he typed in the alarm codes. We passed between giant metal shelves to get to the office. Cold radiated from the bare walls. When I said I needed to pee, he explained which stairs to climb to the toilet on the next floor. I barely found my way back.


The abandoned office wasn’t like him at all. At home, at his place, everything was so cosy. Cushions, leather sofa, lamps. Out there? Desk. Computer. Not even a potted plant.


‘It’s just work,’ he shrugged.


His illness came so suddenly. At first, we thought he’d just caught a cold. It was always cold in that concrete building, that’s why, for Christmas, I bought him a small, portable halogen heater to take with him. He coughed, his back ached.


On the Saturday he helped me do the shopping and then went home to rest. In the evening he called to say that he had a high fever, he was going to see a doctor. I got very scared and I kept on the lookout for any symptoms of my own but felt none.


By Monday he could barely breathe. On Tuesday he was taken in and I packed him a change of pyjamas, a clean towel, cutlery. When I got there, he was sleeping. By the evening the doctor on duty told him that if his condition didn’t improve, on Wednesday they’d sedate him and put him on a ventilator. That’s when he phoned.


It’s difficult to talk about this.


But.


He asked me to take his laptop home from the hospital for him. He dictated the password to me and then fell very silent.


I have to believe him, he kept saying. He had done nothing wrong. Never. Nothing.


He asked me to wipe all the pictures. I shouldn’t look at them, just delete everything that I find. There are two USB drives in his flat, in the left desk drawer. I was to wipe those too, everything.


I’m the only person he can ask.


Of course I looked.


How could I not.


There were young girls in the pictures, lots of naked young girls. I don’t know whether he made all the pictures himself. There were photos of my nieces too. Christ. Réka was standing there in one of them in front of a metal shelf. She had no top on and she was looking into the camera, seductively, like she’d seen in magazines, her nipples stiff from the cold. She was wearing makeup. I think the picture must have been taken in the warehouse, but years ago.


She was still a child, she must have been twelve or thirteen. But among the photos were also children I didn’t know: on the beach, at the playground, in the bath. I saw several series with Alma, on the bicycle, sunbathing, and leaning against some cliff wearing a bandana.


Stark naked.


I don’t know.


I deleted all of them like he asked and I cried. Not just for the girls, for him too. That I had lost someone who was here, and now I was alone with these others whom he’d left here, for me, and whom I didn’t know.


I wanted to tell him I had done what he’d asked, and I wanted to hear him say that this really was everything. I wanted to make him swear he’d only made pictures. It would have meant something to me to hear him say it again.


He never woke up. I was given his phone, and I keep it on me ever since. I can’t visit the girls now, they’re sick.


No, there are no pictures on the phone. He deleted everything, yes.


Occasionally I ring his number, I listen to his voice on the voicemail, and I wonder who he really was.
